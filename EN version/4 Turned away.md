# 4 Turned away

Ah Chen squatted by the stone wall at the entrance of the alley, with the closed door of the relief station beside him.

With his broad knees resting on his chin, he thought of his poor but warm home and his parents who had left together in a tragedy.

When the God of God issued pensions, the deacon's expression was indifferent on the day he delivered the money, as if he were delivering an insignificant letter. The money was just enough for him to stay in that small, tiled-roof house in the alley.

He knew it was the academy's opening day. Maybe it had nothing to do with him, maybe it did, but he was too lazy to tell. He just squatted there quietly, like a still-moving corpse.

Every day he squatted here, watching the crack that climbed up from the base of the wall, which looked like a word, or a dried-up river. He tried to count it; the crack was a little longer today than yesterday, or not, and he could never tell.

There was a creak behind me as the door to the relief station opened a crack, and a boxed lunch was thrown out: "Don't just squat there."

He skillfully reached out and caught it. The steward pulled back, and just as the door was about to close, he tossed out another steamed bun, which rolled to Ah Chen's feet. The bun was already a little deflated from being packed in the paper bag. The steward didn't say anything, and the door closed.

Ah Chen didn't know if it was intentional or accidental. He picked up the steamed bun; it was still warm. He didn't want to think about it, but he ate it anyway.

A familiar vendor pushed his cart past and jokingly said, "Big guy, squatting here again?"

Ah Chen didn't answer, because squatting made him feel at ease, as if he were surrounded by a solid ground beneath his feet.

“My nephew Lin Jing also got in this year,” the vendor said, unable to contain his pride. “That kid has always been a quiet person, but who knew he would step into the realm of spiritual cultivators so quietly.”

Ah Chen's fingertips paused on the lunchbox lid for a moment. Lin Jing—the boy who lived in the deepest part of the alley and only nodded when they met.

Once, someone splashed dirty water in front of him, but he was too lazy to move. Lin Jing walked from the other end of the alley, his footsteps light on the bluestone slabs. He paused, then turned and went into his own yard. A short while later, he came out carrying a half-full bucket of water. The water sloshed violently, a few drops splashing onto his faded trouser legs. He walked to the puddle of dirty water, slid his wrist, and the water rushed out, spreading into a shiny gray streak on the stone slabs. The dirty water, mixed with mud and sand, slowly flowed into the ditch. Lin Jing straightened up, picked up the empty bucket, and walked back. From beginning to end, he didn't glance at A-Chen once.

Ah Chen watched his figure disappear at the end of the alley—just a few more steps in, and there was his own little house with a tiled roof. From beginning to end, Lin Jing hadn't uttered a single word.

Ah Chen didn't reply, but simply opened the lunchbox, took a bite, and chewed slowly.

Seeing that he didn't respond, the vendor waved his hand dismissively, pushed his cart away, and left.

He didn't know what was inside the academy; all he knew was that once he got in, he could escape this dreary existence. He was genuinely happy for Lin Jing, but a heavy weight still pressed on his chest.

He was actually very spiritual, but his mind was clouded by a frantic, chaotic noise that made it almost impossible for him to concentrate on anything. In his youthful ignorance, he thought it was something extraordinary that he could see the well—he didn't yet understand what it meant. The next day, the inspectors arrived.

They dragged him out of bed with swift, decisive movements, like picking up a piece of merchandise awaiting inspection. Someone pried open his eyelids, someone grabbed his chin, someone tore open the back of his collar, and pressed against his spine. He remembers his whole body tensing, his nails digging into his palms, yet he dared not move. That morning, he felt as if he had been taken apart and wouldn't be put back together. Finally, the leader looked at him for a long time and said, "Keep him." Someone nearby asked something, but he didn't answer, turned, and left.

So he was kept. Neither discarded nor used, just watched.

Later, he heard that every child who could see the well saw something different. Some saw flowers, light, and a path paved with stars. But he saw madness, suffering, and some indescribable, ever-churning evil. The well drew closer to him each day, as if it would eventually drag him completely in.

Grandma Lin, who lived at the alley entrance, walked over with her back bent and placed a bowl of hot porridge at his feet: "Drink it, I cooked an extra spoonful of rice this morning."

Grandma Lin is Lin Jing's grandmother. Lin Jing has no parents; after her grandfather passed away, only the grandmother and granddaughter remained, tending to the house at the end of the alley. Ah Chen could sense her soul, though nearing its end, remained clear and pure.

After thanking him, he buried his head in the bowl and sipped the water.

The old woman sat down next to him, leaned against the wall and cried out "Ouch!" to steady herself, then gently patted his shoulder and said, "Ah Jing left today."

“Before he left, the child told me to remember to bring you a bowl of hot porridge every day,” the old woman’s clear voice broke through the thin mist. “He kept repeating the same sentence—'He’s always squatting there, it’s so cold.'”

Ah Chen's Adam's apple bobbed slightly: "He and I... aren't familiar with each other."

“He’s not familiar with anyone,” the old woman smiled, “but he’s always remembered the time you took a punch for him from those thugs at the alley entrance.”

Ah Chen fell silent. Seeing Lin Jing's slender build, the thugs were about to approach when he stepped aside to block them. He took a blow, but they lost interest and left. Lin Jing glanced at him, said thank you, and left.

That morning, Ah Chen woke up and found an old umbrella on the stool by the door. It was neatly folded. He glanced at the other end of the alley; Lin Jing was already gone. The alley entrance was empty, as if no one had ever lived there.

He didn't chase after them, nor did he ask any questions. But he accepted the umbrella and placed it behind the door.

The old woman slowly stood up, walked halfway down the alley, paused for a moment as if she wanted to say something, then continued walking. The wind blew up a few stray white hairs at the back of her neck, but she didn't turn around.

As Ah Chen watched her retreating figure, he recalled that many years ago, when he had just moved to this alley, the first person to ask him "Are you hungry?" was Granny Lin, but back then her back wasn't so bent.

That very night, he lay on the dry bed, listening to the rain on the roof tiles, burying his face in the pillow. The pillow was old and smelled musty. He bit down on a corner, his sobs muffled in his throat. The sound of the rain drowned out everything, and he suddenly felt safe. So he cried more freely, curling up like a wounded animal trying to vomit something out of his throat. In the end, he didn't know if he was crying or just gasping for breath.

He wanted to hate that well. It had ruined most of his life. But each time he returned from the well, the blockage in his chest seemed to soften and slowly melt away, like snowmelt. The well seemed to contain everything, yet also nothing. Once, he saw a pair of hands reaching out from the deepest part of the well, fingertips pointing upwards, as if waiting for someone to pull him up. He reached out, but couldn't reach them. When he woke up, his palms were sweaty.

So he went crazy, trying to recall it over and over again.

He sat on the stone steps for the entire afternoon, as if his soul had been ripped away. He knew that the "quality" of his spirituality was exceptionally pure, but the "quantity" of his spiritual energy was pitifully small. If he sat down and cultivated diligently for just one day, he would achieve a level of progress that an ordinary person couldn't match in a month. But he couldn't control himself—for most of the day, he was dragged back to that well by his ankles. There was no direction in the well, only a feeling that he could never reach the end, urging him to return again and again.

No one told him what it was, and no one told him how to stop.

The beam of light from the direction of the academy began to recede. A distant bell tolled, muffled, as if muffled by several layers of blankets, one after another, causing the stone steps to tremble slightly. Lin Jing was probably being swept into that doorway by the current, like a leaf caught in the wind. From then on, the alley was one less person to wash away his dirty water.

Ah Chen finished the last mouthful of porridge, washed the bowl with clean water, placed it on the low stool by the old woman's door, and whispered through the crack in the door, "Grandma, I've put the bowl here."

The room fell silent for a moment, then the old woman's voice came through the door: "Thank you."

He walked back along the alley. The sound of the bell faded into the distance. A breeze blew in from the alley entrance, carrying the smell of damp earth.

The well reappeared in the depths of my consciousness, like a blurry outline beneath the water's surface, or like a half-open door.

He stopped, leaned against the damp wall, and closed his eyes briefly: "Let me rest for a day, just one day."

There was no answer. He waited a while, the back of his head against the wall, the coolness from the cracks in the bricks slowly seeping into his scalp. Something made a sound from the depths of the alley, then fell silent.

The door wasn't closed, nor was it reopened; it simply remained in place.

Ah Chen opened his eyes and continued walking. The small house with a tiled roof was at the end of the alley. He walked slowly, gently pushed open the creaking wooden door, stepped inside, and shut everything out behind him.

The room was pitch black, lit only by a single, faint flame. The light was weak, just enough to illuminate a low cabinet against the wall. Neatly arranged on the cabinet were two palm-sized wooden plaques, carved by him stroke by stroke. One depicted his father, the other his mother. His father had thick eyebrows, his mother's eyes were curved. He was afraid he would soon forget their faces, so he would take them out to look at them whenever he had a spare moment, each carving feeling like it was etched onto his own body.

He reached for the edge of the bed and sat down, hearing only his own breathing. He didn't know how much time had passed before everything became completely quiet. In the dark depths of his consciousness, something began to rise to the surface, like the tide washing over a beach.

He lay down. Before closing his eyes, his lost past, every inch of land he had ever set foot on, and everything else that was inexplicable and indescribable floated through his mind.

He didn't know why he had to continue living this kind of life. But deep inside, a voice always rose from the bottom of a deep well, like a bubble, whispering something.

He couldn't hear clearly.

But he still lay down and closed his eyes.
