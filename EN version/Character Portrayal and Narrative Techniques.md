#  Character Portrayal and Narrative Techniques

This is an ensemble piece. It doesn't focus on a single individual as the protagonist, but rather presents the multifaceted nature of the world through the eyes of a series of characters. Each character has their own stage; in their own chapter, they are the protagonists, while in others' chapters, they may be supporting characters, passersby, or even enemies.

A cyborg—a creation of brass gears from a maritime civilization. Most of his body has been replaced by psionic machinery; his wooden legs, when disassembled, reveal intricate joint structures. He lives for only one purpose: to explore the end of the deep-sea rift. No one knows why he seeks the light deep within that rift, and he himself cannot explain it. He only knows it is a task that must be completed—an obsession beyond words, like a program with its source code deleted, leaving only the execution instructions spinning aimlessly.

The cultural manager—the quill holder of the manor civilization. She recorded on parchment the trajectory of every gust of wind, the color change of every leaf, the rhythm of every farmer's labor. She believed the world was a poem that needed to be recorded completely, and any omission was an unforgivable dereliction of duty. But the problem was, she recorded so much, yet never truly understood a single thing. She knew the wind's trajectory but not its origin; she knew the color of the leaves but not the name of the tree. Knowledge cannot save humanity; knowledge is merely a tool for survival. And she knew nothing of this.

Commoner Level 1—the man who walked through the grand setting dressed in simple clothes. All respectable psionicists would rather display their profession to show their status, but above his head were only a few words: Commoner Level 1. No one paid him any attention; no one knew why he was there. He might just be a poor soul who had wandered into the wrong place, but he might also be the only truly free soul in this world—because those who don't have a profession are not defined by one. However, most commoners never awaken any profession in their lifetime; they simply constitute the silent majority of this world.

The Faceless Ones—beings that do not belong to any professional system. They walk on the fringes of this world, socially isolated, uncommunicative, and misunderstood. Yet, in every catastrophe, they are always the first to arrive and the last to leave. Their suffering is deeper than anyone else's, but instead of succumbing to it, they transform that pain into the power to protect. Every Faceless One experienced profound heartbreak before becoming one, but that heartbreak did not kill them; instead, it forged them into a form closer to "goodness" itself than humanity.

The Sacred Music Choir—a group of people who use their voices to fight against evil spirits. Legend has it that their voices possess the power to purify spiritual pollution, causing evil spirits to retreat and awakening the lost. They don't sing religious hymns, but rather the oldest and most simple folk melodies—the humming of a mother lulling her child to sleep, the chants of a fisherman hauling in his nets, the short songs sung by miners underground. These melodies contain the joys and sorrows of ordinary people across generations, and it is precisely this most ordinary spiritual resonance that forms the most effective barrier against evil spirits.

These characters walk their own paths, occasionally meeting, occasionally intersecting, and occasionally forming unexpected resonances at crucial junctures. The key to narrative lies in seamless transitions—when switching from one character's perspective to another, the reader doesn't feel abrupt, as if they've always been in the same story, only the camera angle has shifted slightly. This requires restraint: stopping at appropriate points and naturally transitioning to another character's stage, rather than abruptly cutting off or over-interpreting. Each subplot should have a complete ending, allowing the reader to relax before moving on to the next.
