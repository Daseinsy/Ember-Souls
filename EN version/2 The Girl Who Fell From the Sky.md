# 2 The Girl Who Fell From the Sky

In the fiery red depths of the sky, it seemed like an eternally burning flame.

The girl fell into a hallucination once again. In the halo of memory, it was as if a pair of gentle hands were stroking her face, so warm, so reassuring, as if the whole world had become her cradle.

The owner of those hands spoke haltingly, his voice seemingly coming from a distant sky: "Please don't forget..."

For some reason, a damp sadness welled up in her heart—perhaps it was just an illusion.

When Tanaka Yukimi was little, she heard adults say, "Dead beings turn into specks of light and dissipate, returning to the starry sky, like fish swimming into the sea." She couldn't help but wonder, if she had a mother, would her mother be watching over her from the stars in the sky? Occasionally, she would secretly look up at night, trying to identify which star among the myriad stars belonged to her. But the starry sky was vast, each one unfamiliar, each one silent.

Her surname was Tanaka—a common surname for orphans. Her full name was Tanaka Yukimi. No one knew who her parents were. When she woke up, she was lying at the entrance of the orphanage. It was a winter morning, and snow covered her thin swaddling clothes. She was crying, her cries as faint as a cat forgotten in a corner.

She was once an ugly duckling, thin and emaciated from malnutrition. But the madam of the orphanage, with her discerning eye, selected her for the brothel. She was only seven years old that year, even more delicate than her actual age . The madam knelt down, pinched her chin, and looked her over, her gaze devoid of pity, only the calm of an appraiser examining a rough gem. Afterward, she blossomed into an even more beautiful woman, stunningly gorgeous, becoming the white moonlight in countless hearts—pure as snow, melancholic as frost. Of course, she could temporarily sell her art but not her body, but this was merely a temporary measure by the management to sell her for a higher price later.

But she felt fortunate instead—because the Red Mansion was even safer than orphanages or most other areas.

In the orphanage, you were merely a commodity to be picked over at will. The divine authority adhered to the principle of equivalent exchange; murderers who killed ordinary civilians could escape punishment simply by paying a fine, even though the fine was so high that most people couldn't afford it. She had seen people taken away and never return; she had also seen people carried out at night, covered with white sheets, registered, deregistered, and archived by expressionless administrators as if handling a broken object.

In the divine realm, the Red Chamber is a property directly managed by the temple. Every courtesan there is an asset of the divine realm. Many powerful figures frequently come here for pleasure, and killing is strictly forbidden. There, young girls naively fantasize that if they could win the favor of a powerful figure, they could rise to prominence; or young men fantasize about being chosen by a handsome nobleman, and from then on, they would be surrounded by beautiful women and wield power over the world. Some families, driven to desperation, send their children to become courtesans—perhaps for them, the brothel is more comfortable than their natural environment, at least they can eat and dress warmly, have money to spend, and don't have to worry about being taken by sudden evil spirits.

Life at the Red Mansion wasn't entirely bleak. Though the madam was cold, she taught her the zither, music, gait, and speech—all the things that later captivated audiences were honed bit by bit in that small courtyard, through countless days and nights of practice. She remembered her fingers freezing in winter, yet still having to pluck the strings; she remembered her clothes soaked with sweat in summer, yet still practicing until her posture was flawless. At that time, she didn't know what these hardships would become; she only knew that if she didn't practice well, she would be sent back to the orphanage—and she preferred to stay there.

She didn't want to perform, didn't want to live a hypocritical life, but she had no choice. If she had a choice, why would she have chosen this path? But she was too young to do other work, and she couldn't trust anyone.

She was an angelic girl. She was trapped in difficulties countless times, but she always managed to get out—even in such a sad world, she was still willing to believe in the light.

This is perhaps why so many people like her. She seems to possess a strange magnetic force that can firmly attract wounded souls.

People were captivated by her gentleness and vitality on stage, which made her a household name. They especially adored her exquisitely beautiful voice and her plaintive piano melodies, as if she were a fairy untouched by the world. When she sat on stage plucking the strings, the audience would forget to breathe; when she sang, some wept, some were mesmerized, and some knelt on the ground. She herself often wondered—were these people's tears shed for her singing, or for their own unbearable loneliness?

Her fate took a turn.

Children of God's Kingdom are required to learn a wide range of knowledge and skills when they are young, with the goal of cultivating their spirituality. Only when their spirituality has completed its final gestation do they possess the potential to become spiritual practitioners.

Each area has a preaching ground, which is open once a year. A massive divine stele stands in the center of the ground. The stele is a direct derivative of the divine; by releasing spiritual energy and communicating with it, the stele will display different colored areas indicating the corresponding level of spiritual energy. Anyone who reaches the Initial Heart Realm—the lowest level for a spiritual cultivator—before the age of twenty can become a student in the academy for the next academic year.

At the age of sixteen, she successfully entered the realm of initial enlightenment and was directly admitted to the academy, thus completely changing her identity.

When the news arrived, the Red Mansion was in an uproar. The women who usually brushed past her cast complex glances—envy, resentment, and confusion. The madam remained silent for a long time, finally uttering only one sentence: "Go."

The stewards in the Red Mansion treated her differently, showing her concern and even playing the emotional card. They respectfully saw her off and prepared some gifts for her.

However, people still remember her geisha background, and she still has to appear in public wearing a veil.

This was the umpteenth time Tanaka Yukimi had taken the same train, and she only felt exhausted.

People came and went around her, but she couldn't help but marvel at the scenery outside the window, or be attracted by the bizarre world clearly reflected in the glass car window.

She was admiring the scenery, but her spiritual intuition told her that several eyes were watching her—she was beautiful, she knew it perfectly well.

She was once proud of it, but after a while, she felt indifferent. Beauty was a bargaining chip in the Dream of the Red Chamber, a label of value in the eyes of others, but she herself never actively chose its meaning.

In the tinted reflection of the car window, she saw her own face and recalled the madam's expression when she first saw her—a casual glance followed by a sudden halt, a wolf-like gaze fixed on her, as if trying to dissect her features to the very marrow of her bones. In that instant, there was no warmth in the madam's eyes, only a cruel precision. She was terrified then, but didn't understand what that fear meant—later she understood, it was the instinctive tremor of being treated as an object for valuation.

In the blurry reflection, she saw another face that seemed vaguely familiar. He was wearing the same uniform as her, and appeared to be asleep. Even in his sleep, his brows were involuntarily furrowed, his shoulders were tense, and his hands were clenched.

Xuejian had never seen anyone show such an expression in their sleep—sorrow, pain, as if experiencing unimaginable things; tense, alert, relieved, as if piercing through the mist of time and space, only to be ruthlessly thrown back into the sea of people in the mortal world; excruciating pain, but not the kind of physical pain, but the pain of a dying, terminally ill soul still trying to hold on tightly to something.

The old man sitting opposite her suddenly struck up a conversation with her, saying, "Young lady, are you wearing a school uniform on your way to class?"

The mother sitting next to her, who was feeding her child, glanced at her, as if she remembered something, but hesitated to speak.

Xuejian was always a beat slow to react. After regaining her senses, she gently replied, "Yes, Grandpa, I want to go to the academy."

The old man smiled kindly and said, "Ah, I'm an old man. I was a child who fled here when the Divine Kingdom was first established. Now, I don't know many of my peers left. Recently, the Divine Kingdom has been developing so fast that I almost don't understand anything anymore."

The old man suddenly closed his eyes and prayed devoutly, "May the gods bless you."

Xuejian remained silent. She gazed at the old man's wrinkled face, each line like scripture etched by time—etched with what he had believed in his entire life. She wondered if she too would one day become like that, placing all her hopes in a single prayer at the end of her long life.

The train slowed down, and the scenery outside the window changed from wilderness to city. First, a vast expanse of gray-green wasteland receded outside the window, then scattered houses appeared, followed by low walls, chimneys, and markets, and then layers upon layers of buildings and towers.

Against the fiery red backdrop, the outline of the city gradually became clear, like a sleeping beast slowly opening its eyes. The passengers in the carriage began to stir; some got up to retrieve their luggage, others straightened their clothes, and eventually, they disembarked. The entire carriage became quiet; all the other seats were empty, and now only two people remained seated.

As the train neared its final destination, footsteps sounded at the doors. A powerful spiritualist official entered the carriage, scanning the seats one by one. His gaze lingered briefly on Xuejian's veil before shifting to the sleeping boy in the corner.

"Final stop—Inner City Divine Capital." The ticket inspector's voice was mechanical and flat, as if he had repeated it countless times. "Please prepare your documents. You will need to have your spirit runes verified when you exit the gate."

Xuejian took out the temporary pass issued by the academy from her sleeve. The thin piece of paper was covered with a layer of pale golden spirit patterns and felt warm to the touch. She glanced at the boy opposite her—he was awake too, or rather, he had been awake for a while . He was slowly sitting up and pulling out a pass identical to hers from his pocket, his movements devoid of any extra emotion.

The sky outside the window began to darken, and the distant mountain shadows merged into a blurry, deep black.

The ticket inspector nodded and continued walking forward without saying anything, his footsteps gradually fading at the end of the carriage.

The two of them were left.

Xuejian didn't speak. Neither did the boy. The only sounds in the carriage were the rhythmic rumble of the wheels on the tracks and a subtle unspoken understanding—as if both knew that on the final leg of this journey, any words would be superfluous. She wasn't even sure if he was truly awake, because after opening his eyes, he didn't look at her, but gazed out the window at the dark wilderness, as if searching for a hidden star.

The train began to slow. The darkness outside the window was torn apart by light—first a few scattered points, then a continuous expanse. The inner city of the divine realm had arrived. The spirit-patterned lampposts lining the streets lit up simultaneously, their cool, bright blue light stretching to the horizon; shop signs glowed with a warm orange light, and the signal lights atop the towers slowly rotated like the eyes of giants. These light sources converged, illuminating the entire city as if it were a day without sleep, a stark contrast to the wasteland the train had just passed through.

Xuejian stood up and put away her pass. As she walked past the boy, she caught a glimpse of him still sitting there, showing no intention of getting up.

The train door opened, and a rush of warm light and the sounds of people filled the air. The moment Xuejian stepped onto the platform, she looked up—

That thing stood at the very center of the divine realm. A giant formed from pure spiritual light, its translucent body seemingly woven from countless flowing veins of light, tens of feet tall, reaching the dark curtain of the sky. Its face had no features, only a horizontal line of light across it, like an eye that never closes. It stood there motionless, overlooking the entire divine capital, its oppressive aura clearly perceptible even from hundreds of meters away.

Giants of Light—the oversight eyes of the temples—are distributed throughout various regions of the divine realm.

Legend has it that they watch over everything in the divine realm day and night, without ever stopping or overlooking anything.

Xuejian glanced at it only once before looking away. She lowered her head, pulled her veil tighter, and followed the flow of people toward the exit. The hustle and bustle on the platform was irrelevant to her; the cheers, conversations, and calls of names seemed to come from across the water, indistinct and distant.

The nights in the capital were bustling and long, but none of that mattered to her. She spent the night in the temporary inn designated by the academy—the thin door couldn't block out the noise of the street. She lay on her side, her veil still on, listening to the sounds outside in a half-asleep, half-awake state. The spiritual light streaming through the window pierced the thin curtains, casting a pale blue, water-like shadow on the ceiling. She stared at that light until her gaze faded, and her consciousness sank into a dreamless darkness.

When I opened my eyes again, it was already daylight.

The morning air was dry and slightly cool. The academy's outer walls appeared grayish-white in the sunlight, and the spirit-patterned vines were not particularly noticeable in the daylight, simply clinging quietly to the stone walls as if asleep. The open space outside the gate was filled with people—new students, their families, servants carrying luggage, and academy deacons in dark robes—all their voices mingling together in a buzzing cacophony.

She wore a veil, but her figure and demeanor were all too familiar. Most people only marveled at her beauty, while a few recognized her true face. Xuejian could feel those gazes pricking her back like fine needles—not painful, but omnipresent. From a very young age, she had learned to remain composed under such scrutiny—one of the few things the Dream of the Red Chamber had taught her.

"By the way, didn't the Divine Kingdom Daily have a column that mentioned a geisha who was found to have extraordinary spiritual talent during the annual dojo examination and was directly admitted to the academy?"

"That's truly like a phoenix rising from the ashes."

"But won't anyone make fun of her because of her geisha background?"

"What are you thinking? Once she enters the academy, apart from us and those important figures from the divine kingdom, only an ordinary person with a brain full of water would dare to openly laugh at her?" Laughter erupted from the crowd.

One young lady couldn't stand it any longer. She stepped forward, put her hands on her hips, and angrily said to the group of male students, "Is it fun to laugh at others? Who do you think you are? Cowards who only dare to talk big but never take any action."

The air fell silent for a moment. The smiles on the male students' faces froze, as if someone had grabbed their throats. The young lady, however, glared at them relentlessly, her eyes devoid of condescending superiority, filled only with a pure, almost fiery, anger.

Tanaka Yukimi spotted a familiar young man. He possessed a melancholic air, standing alone in the crowd, just like her. He stood at the very edge of the crowd, as if ready to withdraw from the commotion at any moment. The instant the young lady spoke, he slightly turned his head and glanced in that direction—not to observe the spectacle, but as if confirming something. Then he looked away, as still as a stagnant lake.

He didn't change his clothes. He was still wearing the same uniform as yesterday, with nothing hanging from his shoulders.

Xuejian withdrew her gaze and stopped looking at him. She simply stood there, waiting for the order to assemble.

The boy stood at the far end of the crowd, silently like her, not speaking to anyone.
