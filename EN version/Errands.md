# Errands

Delivery runners are widespread because they are cheap and inexpensive to move.

Now, there was this old man, whose name was Santiago. He sailed alone in a small boat, making a living by delivering goods in the Gulf Stream. But the sail he carried was in a terrible state—upon closer inspection, it was revealed to be patched together from countless torn sacks of coarse hemp and flour, a jumble of colors—blue, white, yellow, and brown—densely stitched with dozens of patches of coarse thread. Now rolled up, it hung heavily on the mast, looking less like a fisherman's attire and more like the tattered banners dragged by a defeated general of ancient times, hastily looking north, exuding boundless desolation and bleakness.

Now, this old man, on this voyage, has already been at sea for eighty-four days. Eighty-four days is neither long nor short, but if he hasn't seen a single big order, the feeling is like chewing wax, tormenting him day after day.

For the first forty days, a child accompanied him. The child was named Manolin, and was clever and quick-witted. He had lost his father at a young age, and the old man had taught him to recognize wind direction, observe tides, and identify schools of fish. Although they were master and apprentice, their relationship was like that of grandfather and grandson. However, those were unlucky days. Forty days and nights passed in vain, and the cabin remained empty , without even a fish scale. The child's parents couldn't bear it any longer. They sighed countless times in private and believed the gossip of the idle men in the village, who said that the old man must have encountered some sea evil spirit, and that his soul was carrying the bad luck of "salao," and that they must not let their child associate with him anymore. So one day, the parents called the child to their side and sternly admonished him, "If you continue to follow that loser, you might not even get a bowl of thin porridge. The boat owner next door is having a stroke of good luck; he made a full hold on his first day at sea. You should go and join him; that's the right thing to do." The child was young, and although he was extremely reluctant, he dared not disobey his parents' strict orders. He could only pack his bags with tears in his eyes and board another boat. As luck would have it, in the first week, the child helped the new boat owner catch three large fish, each weighing over a hundred pounds. The villagers were all amazed, saying, "A tree dies when transplanted, but a person thrives."

Yet, the child's heart remained preoccupied with the old man. Every evening at dusk, he would stand at the bow of his own boat and watch from afar as the old man's small boat slowly rowed back from the horizon. The boat had a very shallow draft, floating lightly on the water like a fallen leaf, clearly indicating that the hold was empty. When it reached the shore, the old man would furl the sail and tie the mooring lines, his movements slow and his back hunched, as if he had exhausted all his strength. The child's heart would feel as if it were bound by a rope. He would rush forward, saying little, silently helping the old man gather the tangled pile of fishing lines—thick as a thumb, hard and rough to the touch after being exposed to the sun and water; he would also remove the cast-iron fishhooks, polish the gleaming harpoons, and finally carefully untie the patchwork sail, fold it neatly, wrap it tightly around the mast, and tie it securely with old rope. The father and son worked together seamlessly. Though they didn't speak, a warm affection between them flowed through the twilight.

The old man's appearance was truly pitiful and withered. He was so thin that only bones remained, and deep wrinkles, like ravines, were etched on the back of his neck by years of direct sea winds and scorching sun. His cheeks were covered with brown spots, the result of years of burning from the intense sunlight reflecting off the tropical sea surface—medically known as benign skin tumors. They were painless and didn't itch, but like patina, they spread along his cheeks to his ears, staining his once dark skin mottled. Most striking were his hands—his palms and the base of his fingers were covered with deep scars from a lifetime of pulling on rough fishing lines, horizontal and vertical lines crisscrossing each other. These scars were not new; their color had long since faded, and they felt smooth and dry to the touch, like the sand and rock layers in a fishless desert eroded by countless winds and sands over thousands of years, exuding a deathly, aged air. From head to toe, his entire being showed signs of decay. Yet, the strangest things in the world often happen here—his eyes, however, remained youthful. The color of his eyes was exactly the same as the boundless blue sea before him, a deep, clear blue, without a trace of cloudiness in his pupils. Instead, they constantly shone with a childlike joy, and a stubborn, unyielding light that would never bow down no matter how many setbacks he suffered.

That day, the two men packed up their boat and set off from the rocky shore toward the village. The boy, being young, couldn't keep his thoughts to himself. As they walked, he tugged at the old man's sleeve, looked up at him, and said, "Santiago, I can go out to sea with you again. My family has earned some money this time, we don't have to worry about food and drink anymore, and my parents can't be so strict with me." This was the boy's sincere heart; he couldn't bear to sever the debt of gratitude the old man had shown him, simply because the old man had taught him how to earn a living. Hearing this, the old man stopped and turned his head. A gentle light flashed in his sea-blue eyes, but he waved his scarred hand and said, "No, don't say that. The ship you're on is having a lucky streak; you should stick with it wholeheartedly. Fortune in the sea is unpredictable; how can you change it so easily?" The child became anxious and hurriedly said, "But you should remember that summer when you didn't catch a single fish for eighty-seven days straight, and everyone in the village laughed at you, saying you were crazy. But then what happened? For three weeks in a row, we were able to haul back a thousand-pound marlin every day! Back then, you taught me how to steer and how to navigate by looking at the stars at night. Have you forgotten?" The old man nodded, his voice hoarse but calm: "I remember, I remember it clearly. So I know..." "You left me not because you didn't trust me, but because your parents made you leave. You had no choice." The child lowered his head, grinding the broken seashells on the beach with his toes, and muttered, "My father made me leave. I'm just a child; I couldn't disobey him." "I know," the old man reached out and awkwardly patted the child's shoulder, "It's only natural. You can't blame yourself, nor can you blame your parents." The child suddenly looked up again, his eyes filled with fervent expectation: "But he didn't have faith in you, but we have faith in ourselves, right? We have faith in ourselves!" The old man looked at his anxious face and couldn't help but smile slightly. That smile touched the deep wrinkles on the back of his neck, appearing both desolate and warm: "Yes, we have faith. We have plenty."

The child cheered up and took the old man's hand, saying, "In that case, let me invite you to the terrace bar for a beer to relax, and then we can carry your things home." The old man wanted to decline, but seeing how enthusiastic the child was, he couldn't bear to refuse. He simply waved his hand heartily and laughed, "Why not? We're all brothers who fish, having a drink to warm ourselves up is only right." With that, the two walked side by side into the twilight towards the riverside tavern where the lights were just beginning to twinkle.
