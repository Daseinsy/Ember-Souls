#  All urged on

Lin Jing is most afraid of being rushed by things.

They urged him to get up, to leave the house, to do anything that seemed simple to others. Each time that urging sounded, he felt something chasing him even closer. That thing had no shape, no color, no smell, but Lin Jing knew it was always there. Like a black dog, silently and tirelessly following at his heels.

He sat on the creaking edge of the bed in his rented room; the daylight outside was already bright. His psionic communication was with his roommate, Chen Chen. The message was short: "Going today?"

There was no question mark. It wasn't even a prompt, just a question. But Lin Jing felt the blurry, swirling mist in his chest suddenly accelerate. His spirit surged wildly between his ribs, a frantic, persistent, and undeniable alarm.

Whether to go or not is a difficult question.

He knew where he was going today. A flea market in the suburbs; Chen Chen said there was a stall there selling old items. Lin Jing didn't like it. Those old things always carried the scent of others, their stories, their sorrows. They were like dense spiderwebs, entangled around his ankles. But he couldn't refuse them. He didn't know why he wanted to refuse.

How ironic. Those eyes let him see so much, yet they never let him close them.

A breeze blew in through the window. The curtains swayed gently, and sunlight cast a shimmering shadow on the floor. Lin Jing suddenly remembered something he'd seen on the bus yesterday. A middle-aged woman sat opposite him, clutching an old canvas bag, half a faded red scarf peeking out. Her fingers constantly caressed the scarf, her eyes fixed on the window, her lips moving slightly as if she were reciting something. As the bus turned, sunlight streamed in through the window, illuminating her profile. Lin Jing saw her sadness. No, not saw, but felt. That sadness was like a thin, transparent river, flowing from her, washing over him, and then continuing on into the distance. He even knew who she was; he had no basis for it, but he just knew. That knowledge was like a needle, stuck in his heart, impossible to pull out.

When he got out of the car, the woman's sadness still clung to his skin.

Chen Chen once said that Lin Jing's condition is called "excessive empathy," which is a psychological problem and requires seeing a doctor. But no doctor can understand it.

Lin Jing looked down at her hands. Her fingers were still trembling slightly, but the trembling had lessened. The spiritual alarm was still ringing, only now it had become muffled, less urgent, and more drawn-out, like some kind of background noise, forever and incessantly humming there.

He stood up and walked to the window.

On the path downstairs, an old woman was walking a dog. The dog was black, small, and followed behind the old woman with tiny, shuffling steps. Lin Jing's gaze fell on the dog, and suddenly she felt a chill run down the back of her neck.

He turned around, grabbed his coat draped over the back of the chair, and pushed open the door.

In the living room, Chen Chen was reclining on the sofa, flipping through a thick picture book. Hearing the noise, he raised his eyelids slightly: "Gone?"

"I'm leaving," Lin Jing said.

Chen Chen closed his book, sat up from the sofa, and reached for his car keys on the coffee table. His movements were swift and decisive, without any hesitation. Lin Jing sometimes envied this about Chen Chen—his ability to do what he wanted and leave when he wanted. Chen Chen's world was like a wide, straight road, while his world was a bumpy, narrow path.

"The stall owner said he had a Ming Dynasty bronze mirror that was in good condition, except for some patina on the back," Chen Chen said while putting on his shoes. "I looked at the photo, and the pattern should be a sea creature and grape motif, but I'm not sure. Could you take a look for me?"

"I don't understand."

“You can understand it.” Chen Chen straightened up and smiled at him. “You can understand everything.”

Lin Jing didn't reply. He recalled the first time Chen Chen took him to the antique market. They had only known each other for a short time then, and Chen Chen assumed he liked those things because Lin Jing would always stand for a long time in front of a particular stall, staring intently at an object. Chen Chen didn't know that Lin Jing was simply looking at what was behind those objects. Those things were like undercurrents beneath the surface of water, like cracks behind a wall. Each old object held a piece of time, a piece of life, a story that no one had ever spoken aloud.

Lin Jing can see it.

For example, that bronze mirror. When Chen Chen handed him the photo, the moment his fingers touched the screen, he felt a sense of oppression. Not from the mirror itself, but from its owner. A woman, a woman dressing in front of a mirror. Her hair was long, and as she combed it, she cried. On the back of the bronze mirror were grapes, sea creatures, and entwined vines. Those patterns were like some kind of incantation, dragging Lin Jing into an era he didn't recognize.

But Chen Chen was urging him.

"Let's go, or the good ones will be taken by someone else."

Chen Chen's urging was light and natural, like a wad of cotton. But to Lin Jing, it sounded like someone had suddenly shoved him from behind. The black dog's breath sprayed onto his ankle.

He opened the door and went out.
