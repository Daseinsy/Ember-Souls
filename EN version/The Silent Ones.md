# The Silent Ones

The artificial sun shines precisely from the east, like a giant, never-blinking pupil.

Ah Chen was awake. He lay on the lower bunk, listening to the groaning of the metal frame as the person on the upper bunk turned over. The walls were thin, and the sound of water flushing the toilet in the next room came through the pipes, like something vomiting in the dark. His eyes were fixed on the thin crack in the ceiling, stretching from the corner of the wall to the light fixture, like a dried-up river.

He counted. One thousand four hundred and sixty-two days.

From the day he was assigned to the Seventh Welfare Zone, the artificial sun shone 1,462 times. Each day was precise, like a graduation line, dividing the rest of his life. He was eighteen years old, with no physical illnesses. His mental health assessment report stated: Normal. Socialization score: Below average. Potential for contribution: To be observed.

Ah Chen sat up, his head hitting the upper bunk with a dull thud. The person in the upper bunk rolled over but didn't wake up. He bent down to put on his shoes; one of the shoelaces had broken, the break rough, indicating it had worn through last month. He tied a tight knot and continued putting them on.

The cafeteria was on the second basement level. The air was thick with the smell of synthetic protein and disinfectant, like an oily film covering the throat. The line was long, everyone dressed in gray standard uniforms, looking like a line of faceless shadows from behind. Ah Chen carried his tray forward; today's ration consisted of a piece of compressed cereal, a spoonful of protein paste, and half a cup of warm water. He found a corner to sit down, facing the wall.

There are words on the wall.

Someone carved it with their fingernail or some other sharp object, crookedly: I want to see the real sun.

Ah Chen stared at the words, his spoon hovering in mid-air. He recognized the words; it hadn't been here last month. The person who sat in this seat last month had been taken away; apparently, he'd suddenly stood up during a nighttime presentation and shouted something. Ah Chen hadn't heard the words himself, but later the whole welfare area was buzzing with the rumor that the person had shouted, "That one in the sky is fake."

The guards of the divine kingdom reacted quickly, pinning him down within three seconds, gagging him, and dragging him out. Since then, the corner of the cafeteria has been repainted, and the current inscription is newly carved, with deep engravings that feel rough to the touch.

Ah Chen withdrew his finger and swallowed the last mouthful of egg white paste. It couldn't taste anything; it was like chewing on a lump of warm mud.

The attendance bell rang.

The workshop lights were brighter than the cafeteria lights, so bright there were no shadows. Ah Chen's job was to visually inspect the parts on the assembly line, forty pieces per minute, with an error rate not exceeding three per thousand. The conveyor belt in front of him never stopped, and the silver-gray metal blocks flowed past his eyes like a silent river. His fingers didn't need to think; only his eyes moved.

Uncle Lin, who works at the next workstation, occasionally speaks. He is an old man in the seventh welfare area, over fifty years old, with an old scar on his face that runs from his brow bone to his jaw. Every time he turns his head, the scar twitches with his muscles, like a centipede crawling under his skin.

"I heard another one died in District Nine." Uncle Lin's voice was very low, his lips barely moving, a habit he had developed over the years.

Ah Chen didn't look up; his eyes were fixed on the conveyor belt.

"How did you die?" he asked, his voice equally low.

"An investigation," Uncle Lin said. "They said there was an inaccurate wording in one of the sentences in his self-criticism report. He was detained for two days and suffered cardiac arrest."

A part on the conveyor belt had a minor scratch on its surface. Ah Chen picked it out and put it in the defective product box. His movements were clean and efficient, and the error rate remained at one in a thousand.

"Did his family receive compensation?" Achen asked.

"Compensation?" Uncle Lin's lips twitched. "His parents passed away last year. He's all alone. The district didn't even issue an obituary."

The conveyor belt continues to flow. Forty pieces. Forty pieces. Forty pieces.

During his lunch break, Ah Chen didn't go to the canteen. He walked to the end of the corridor outside the workshop, where there was a sealed window. He looked out through the crack in the glass. The artificial sun hung in the center of the divine realm, bright, stable, and without the slightest fluctuation. All the buildings were bathed in that uniform light; no shadows were stretched, no angles changed. Like an overexposed photograph, everything was there, but everything seemed unreal.

He remembered that when he was a child, in the largest activity room of the orphanage, the teacher showed them a film. It was footage from before the Great Cataclysm, from a place that no longer exists. In the video, a sun slowly sank below the horizon, its color changing from incandescent white to orange-yellow, and then to a blood-red. That red spread across half the sky, like it was burning.

Ah Chen cried when he saw it.

The children around him laughed at him. The teacher patted his head and said, "It's okay, this is a normal emotional reaction."

He later learned that the teacher had been transferred the very next day. Reason: Showing unapproved historical footage during children's education.

Now he stood before the sealed window, staring at the artificial sun overhead that would never set, his eyes aching. He knew the pain was physical, unrelated to emotion. But he vaguely wished he could cry like he did as a child. He couldn't cry.

That night, after the lights were turned off, Ah Chen lay in the darkness, listening to the breathing of the entire building. The person in the upper bunk was talking in their sleep, intermittently, like reciting a prayer. Ah Chen made out a few words: Please, let me out.

He rolled over and faced the wall.

There were words carved on the wall. Not yesterday, but today. Right next to his pillow, the words seemed to grow from the gray plaster, small and deep, each mark carrying an almost frenzied force. He leaned closer to look; there were only three lines:

Don't speak.

I'm listening.

They were listening too.

Achen stared at the three lines of text and felt his heartbeat suddenly become very loud, as if someone was pounding an invisible drum with their fist inside his chest.

He didn't move. He didn't even reach out to touch the carvings. He just lay there with his eyes open, feeling himself alive in the darkness—his heart beating, his lungs breathing, his blood circulating in his veins. He felt these things, and he felt something else: a silent, restless fear surging in from all directions. Others' fears, his own, all mixed together.

He felt like a taut stringed instrument, and the slightest breeze could make it tremble uncontrollably.

Ah Chen thought that as long as he could live healthily, everything was still negotiable. Death meant the end. Life wasn't a single-plank bridge, it was a marathon. A short-term lead didn't count as winning; only reaching the end steadily was true victory. But this world felt like a dream he couldn't wake from. He was running on this endless track, with no finish line ahead and no pursuers behind. Only that false sun, forever hanging in the sky, shone on him, and on everyone else.

He closed his eyes.

In his dream, he saw a real sun sinking below a distant horizon. The sky was ablaze. He opened his mouth, as if to shout something to someone. But there was no sound in the dream, and he had no mouth. He was only a pair of eyes, witnessing that magnificent, forbidden sunset.

When I woke up, the artificial sun was already shining.
