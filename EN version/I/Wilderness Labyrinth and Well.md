#  Wilderness, Labyrinth and Well

The wilderness is the most awe-inspiring place in the phenomenal world. Not because it is perpetually dark, nor because evil spirits frequently roam—but because of its unfathomable silence and mystery. You wander there, with no direction in sight, often losing your way and becoming trapped and dying within. The most chilling aspect is the desolate loneliness of utter annihilation. Shouting aloud brings no response, and the very act of hearing a response is all the more chilling—you don't know what it is: a human, a non-human echo, or perhaps just the inexplicable rustling of the wind through the grass.

The greatest danger in the wilderness is not darkness or evil spirits, but the "sorrow" that erodes spirits. There is a time limit for staying in the wilderness; after a certain period, spirits are assimilated by the wilderness, either transforming into evil spirits or monsters. Therefore, explorers entering the wilderness must carry a timer—not to record time, but to calculate how much "spiritual quota" they have left. Some experienced wilderness hunters will return every so often, like divers surfacing for air, using the city-state's fire to reset their spiritual state.

Scattered across the wilderness are the ruins of once-glorious civilizations. These ruins belong to even earlier civilizations—some architectural styles are completely unrecognizable, and some inscriptions are beyond the decipherability of any descendant. The settlements vary greatly: some are utterly abandoned, their weathered walls the only remnants; others have been taken over by evil spirits, becoming their new lair; still others appear orderly, but are in reality breeding grounds set up by evil gods—people live within walls, believing themselves free, when in fact they are merely livestock. A small minority manage to migrate from these wilderness settlements to city-states, but they, in turn, oppress those still struggling in the wilderness, just as they themselves were oppressed in the past.

The Labyrinth is a gateway to various worlds. These gateways are hidden deep in the wilderness or in mist-shrouded areas, and no one knows where they lead. Legend has it that at the end of the Labyrinth lies an ultimate demon—a powerful being at the Sovereign Realm once entered the Labyrinth and never returned. Therefore, all the great empires have marked the Labyrinth as a red zone, deploying armies to seal it off at every level. But there are always those who are curious, always those who think, "Perhaps I will be the exception." Those people usually only have one fate: to become new material for legends.

Legend has it that children with exceptionally strong spiritual abilities dream of a well. This well is endlessly deep, its water as smooth as a mirror, yet the reflections are always distorted. In their dreams, the child stands beside the well, looking down not at their own face, but at the silhouette of a stranger, or a blurry form of countless overlapping figures. These dreams recur, sometimes accompanied by severe headaches, auditory hallucinations, and visual hallucinations. These children are called "Children of the Well."

What exactly is the well? No one knows the exact answer. Some say it's a crack in the world's consciousness, some say it's the remains of ancient gods, some say it's the connection between the mirror world and the phenomenal world. But one thing is certain: once you step into this well, there is no turning back. It's like fate itself—you can only stand on the edge and watch, you can't jump in. Those who jump in either become madmen, saints, or faceless beings.

The image of the well permeates the deepest recesses of this world. Every evil spirit was once a dead one, and every boundary marker bears the inscription of a tragedy. The sorrow stems from having seen wells so incredibly deep. And the children of the well are precisely the most hopeful beings of this era—because they can still feel that sorrow that transcends the individual, almost a sorrow directed at fate. As long as someone dreams of that well, as long as someone lingers by its edge, gazing and questioning, this world has not truly died.

Spirituality never dries up; it is merely worn away by suffering. But the spirit is like well water—as long as the earth still has life, it will always spring up again after a drought.
