# TwilightSeats

There are no stars above the dome.

The dome of the Twilight Council Hall was a frozen sea, its deep gray waters suspended above everyone's heads. Occasionally, enormous shadows would drift through it, slow and silent, like something unwilling to be identified. No one looked up at those shadows. Everyone present knew what they were—the gaze of the Holy Spirits, an eternal, emotionless stare.

Veranos sat at the eastern end of the long table, his fingertips lightly tapping the surface. His throne no longer needed a physical form, but out of some ancient ritualistic sense, he still chose to sit in human form. The runes woven into his golden robes flickered with his breath, each rune corresponding to a still-functioning temple in the lower realm. Today, three fewer temples were lit up than last month. He noticed, but showed no sign of it.

"You're late, Athero."

"I'm never late." The voice came from the other end of the long table, carrying a smile that was hard to tell if it was sarcasm or sincerity. Aithro's figure materialized in the air, like a painting soaked by rain, its outline constantly shifting, never settling on a single point. Finally, he chose a young face—too young, almost offensively so. "I just stopped by the market in Morion on my way here. Quite interesting. Your followers are singing my hymns, Veranos. Of course, they don't know whose melody it is."

Veranos's fingers paused for a moment.

"Cultural infiltration," he said calmly, as if stating the weather. "Over three years, through trade routes, drinking songs, and festive customs, you gradually peeled away the folk beliefs of Morion from my system of order. There were no manifestations, no miracles, no direct interference with the free will of mortals."

"You see, this is why I love the new era." Athero finally sat down, his posture as casual as if he were in his own backyard. "What did we do in the past? Thunder, floods, plagues—we smashed the enemy's temple to rubble. And then?"

His gaze suddenly sharpened, but the smile on his lips did not disappear.

"And then Ragnarok came."

These words fell into the silence at the center of the long table, like a stone falling into a deep pool.

The sea above my head rippled slightly.

Veranos didn't reply. He didn't need to—everyone present remembered. The embers of that final battle were still embedded in the skeleton of the world: the frost in the north had never truly melted, the fissures in the abyss continued to slowly expand, and a third of the stars remained forever extinguished. It wasn't a page in a history book, but a scar etched deep within the divine essence of every god.

"So we sit here," Veranos finally spoke, his voice low and steady. "Instead of thunder, instead of iron cavalry, instead of hymns; instead of horses, instead of trade routes. You may continue, but you cannot cross the Morion. That is my bottom line."

"Bottom line." Athero chewed on the word, as if savoring a new dish.

On the third side of the long table, the darkness was even more intense than usual.

Nerekara did not appear in human form. She was a slowly writhing mass of dark existence, with tentacle-like structures constantly emerging and dissolving from her edges. Occasionally, the outlines of countless eyes would appear on her surface, only to quickly disappear again. Her breathing was like the tides, like the turning over of some enormous creature deep underground.

"You are vying for the ownership of a demigod." Her voice didn't travel through the air, but resounded directly within the consciousness of the other two, damp and heavy. "I smell the blood of Morion's territory. That demigod lord—Morion—he has begun to waver. Things that shouldn't be appearing are appearing in his dreams."

"You care about Morion Territory?" Athero turned his head to look at the darkness.

"I don't care about territory," Nerekara replied, like a bubble rising from the deep sea. "I care about balance. The two of you—order and cunning—have been locked in a struggle for seventy years in Morion. Seventy years of cultural infiltration, seventy years of covert power struggles. The spirit of that demigod is being torn apart by your two forces. If he collapses, the cracks will widen. And as the cracks widen, the things of the abyss will seep out."

One of her eyes opened in the darkness, cloudy and huge, staring directly at Athero.

"You should know better than I what's in the crack."

Aithro's smile froze for a moment—just a moment—before returning to normal. But Veranos noticed.

"Nerekara is right," Veranos continued, his tone unchanged. "Morian's mental state is the bottom line. He can waver in his faith, he can switch allegiances, but he cannot collapse. The mental breakdown of a demigod will tear open a portal in the mortal realm, and once that portal is open—"

"The Holy Spirits will intervene," Ethero finished for him, the laughter finally gone from his voice.

The three of them fell silent.

The sea above rippled once more. This time, everyone felt the weight of that gaze—not anger, not a warning, but a pure, mechanical, and irresistible scrutiny. The Holy Spirits need no emotions. They are the very infrastructure of this world: gravity, light, the flow of time, the workings of causality. If the struggle between the gods threatens these foundations, the Holy Spirits will not hesitate to sever everything—cut off the supply of divine power, sever the channels of faith, sever every causal chain upon which the gods depend for their existence.

The last time a god attempted to break the rules was at the end of the Third Age. The fate of that god of war was not death, but rather "never existed"—his name was erased from all memory, his temple became an empty space that was never built, and even other gods had to consult the oldest archives to recall that such a being had ever existed.

That wasn't punishment. That was correction.

"Therefore," Veranos broke the silence, placing his hands flat on the table, "I propose a demarcation. West of the Morion River is my traditional religious territory; your cultural infiltration ends there. East of the Morion River is your free zone of infiltration; I will not interfere. As for Morion himself—"

"He wants to choose for himself," Nerekara interrupted him.

Veranos frowned.

"Demigods have free will." The Abyss Mother's tentacles slowly extended, tracing irregular arcs in the darkness. "You can influence him, but you cannot decide for him. This is the rule."

"The rules." Athero smiled again, but this time there was a hint of bitterness in his smile. "When did we become slaves to the rules?"

"From the day we almost destroyed ourselves."

These were the words spoken by Nerekara. Her countless eyes closed and reopened simultaneously, as if performing some ancient ritual.

"Do you think I want to sit here and negotiate with you?" Her voice suddenly took on an ancient weariness. "I am the Mother of the Abyss. I could have let the rift widen, allowing the things of the past to return. But what if the things of the past return? They don't know me, and I don't know them. This world may be broken, but at least it's still ours."

The long table remained silent for a long time.

"I accept your proposal," Athero spoke first, leaning back with a relaxed air. "The land west of Morion is yours. But you must guarantee one thing—your priests will no longer burn my hymns in the name of 'purifying heresy.' My men have already filed a complaint about what happened in Riverdale last month."

"Those hymns bear the imprint of your divine power," Veranos replied calmly. "Mortals, in the process of reciting them, unconsciously deviate from the structure of the ordered prayers. This is not cultural infiltration; it is the pollution of faith."

"You call it pollution, I call it evolution."

"I said it's pollution."

Their gazes met above the long table, and tiny cracks flickered in the air—the marks of divine power colliding unintentionally.

The sea above us rippled violently.

Both of them simultaneously withdrew their gaze. The crack disappeared.

"...I will inform the priests to be mindful of their methods," Veranos said. This was the maximum concession he could make.

"That's enough." Athero shrugged.

A low sigh echoed from the darkness of Nerekara, like the final eruption of an underwater volcano.

"And as for Morion himself—" she said slowly, "I will send my messengers into his dreams. Not to interfere, but simply… to observe. If his mental state falls below a critical threshold, I will inform the Holy Spirits directly. You all know the consequences."

This is not a threat. This is a statement.

Veranos nodded. Aithro raised his hand, like a casual student in class.

"There's one more thing," Veranos said, turning his gaze to the perpetually silent shadow at the end of the long table—where sat the fourth attendee, who hadn't uttered a single syllable from beginning to end.

The shadow leaned slightly forward.

"The rift in the north has recently expanded by seventeen miles," Veranos's voice took on a somber tone for the first time. "Frost is spreading. This is not a natural phenomenon."

silence.

"Someone has violated the agreement," he said. "Someone is conducting a massive drain of divine power near the rift, accelerating the spread of the frost. If this continues, the North will collapse on its own, without even needing Ragnarok."

His gaze swept over everyone present.

"I need an answer. Who is it?"

Aethro's smile vanished. Nerekara's tentacles ceased their twitching. The shadow of the fourth attendee grew even darker.

A huge shadow swam across the sea overhead once more.

This time, everyone raised their heads.

Chapter 8: Morion's Dream

He dreamed of that river again.

The Morion River. Its greyish-green surface, like a crumpled piece of silk, flowed slowly and irresistibly eastward in the moonlight. He stood on the west bank, his boots sinking into the cold, wet mud, his toes numb. The river was silent—this wasn't right. He remembered this river had sounds: water lapping against pebbles, reeds rustling in the wind, the sound of night birds' wings cutting through the air as they skimmed across the water. But now all that was gone, leaving only a silent, flowing, vast grey expanse.

Then he heard singing.

It came from the east bank. Far, yet unreasonably close, as if the sound wasn't traveling through space, but rather growing directly inside his skull. The melody was unfamiliar—no, not entirely unfamiliar. He felt he must have heard it at some festival, on a harvest night, around a campfire, people raising their mead and singing in a language he couldn't remember. He couldn't recall the lyrics, but the melody, like a thread, had coiled around a rusty string in his chest.

He wanted to walk over there.

The mud beneath his feet suddenly softened. No, not softened—it melted. The mud turned into water, the water into mist, and the mist into something thinner than mist. He was sinking. Not falling, but like a coin being slowly pressed into the water, the world closing in on him.

The singing has changed.

It was no longer a festive melody. It had become ancient, so ancient that it didn't belong to any known language system. There were unnatural gaps between the syllables, as if the speaker—or rather, the singer—had to think about too many things at once, so that each word was fragmented. In his half-awake state, Morion realized that there was a certain structure embedded in the song, a structure that he couldn't decipher with mortal cognition, like trying to understand a three-dimensional object with two-dimensional eyes.

He saw it.

The silt at the bottom of the river churned, something was beneath it. Not fish, not water plants, not anything belonging to this river. It was a mass… He couldn't find the words. It was a mass darker than darkness itself, its edges constantly negating themselves, as if it existed in all directions yet was not in any direction at all. It was breathing. With each breath, Morion could feel his divinity—the demigod mark embedded deep within his soul—being clenched tighter by an invisible hand.

Then he woke up.

The bedroom ceiling was made of oak and engraved with the Morion family crest—a deer carrying an ear of wheat. He stared at the deer for a long time, until his eyes ached, until he was sure it wouldn't move, wouldn't blink, and wouldn't come down from the wood.

It was a Morion morning outside the window. The grey-blue light of dawn seeped through the gaps in the curtains, carrying the damp scent unique to the valley. In the distance, the sounds of the market opening could be heard—wheels rolling over the cobblestones, vendors' shouts, and the low mooing of livestock. Everything was normal. Everything was perfectly normal.

But his back was wet.

It wasn't sweat. It was a sticky, cold liquid, as if something had seeped from his skin while he slept. He reached up and touched it; his fingertips were coated with a layer of transparent gel, gleaming faintly in the morning light with a strange, otherworldly blue sheen.

He pulled his hand back and wiped it on the sheets.

"grown ups."

The door was pushed open a crack. It was his attendant, a middle-aged man named Herren, with an old scar running from his forehead to his chin. Herren carried a basin of hot water and a clean linen cloth. His gaze lingered on Morion's hands for a moment, but he said nothing.

"What time is it?"

"It's just past Chen Shi (7-9 AM)." Helen placed the basin of water on the low table beside the bed. "The caravan from River Valley has arrived. As you instructed yesterday, we need to meet their leader today. And—" He hesitated for a moment, "The High Priest sent word this morning that you are required to attend the morning prayers at the temple."

Which priest?

"Calvin, the priest of Lord Veranos."

Morion didn't answer immediately. He looked at his hand—the hand that had just touched his back. A faint blue tinge remained under his fingernails, almost invisible in the morning light, but he knew it was there.

Tell Calvin I'll arrive at noon.

"Yes." Herren turned to leave.

"Helen."

The attendant stopped in his tracks.

What's the latest news from the north?

Herren's expression shifted slightly. It wasn't surprise, but rather an unease stemming from a premonition that had been confirmed.

"The scouts reported three days ago that the frost in the northern fissures has expanded further. It's about seventeen li more than last month." He paused. "Some farmers in the territory have started talking about it, saying they can hear a sound in the north wind at night. It doesn't sound like the wind."

What was that sound?

Herren opened his mouth, then closed it again. He was a pragmatic man who had taken three fatal wounds for Morion on the battlefield, but at this moment his expression was like that of a child being asked to describe something he was certain existed but could not prove in words.

"It's like... something breathing," he finally said, his voice low, "but not human breathing. It's too loud."

Morion nodded without asking any further questions.

After Herren left the room, he sat alone for a long time. The deer emblem on the oak ceiling watched him quietly in the morning light, just as it did every morning.

But he suddenly felt that the deer's eyes were different from yesterday's.

The market in Riverdale stretched out in an open area three miles west of the River Morion, extending nearly half a mile along the riverbank. As Morion rode by, he noticed a detail: the best-selling items at the market were not grain or cloth, but collections of hymns.

A thin parchment booklet, its cover printed with unfamiliar melodic symbols, crudely bound yet selling like hotcakes. A middle-aged woman selling ale was humming a tune from it to her son, her voice muffled and off-key, but the melody was unmistakable—

He reined in his horse.

That melody. The melody from my dream last night.

He sat on his horse, pausing for three seconds amidst the bustling market. The surrounding noise seemed to be blocked by a thin membrane; he could hear his own heartbeat, the horse's snorting, and the woman's off-key singing. Three seconds later, the membrane vanished, and everything returned to normal.

"My lord?" The captain of the accompanying guard rode up to them.

"It's nothing." Morion spurred his horse and continued toward the meeting place in the center of the market.

But he remembered the melody of that song. Every single note.

The caravan leader was a short, stocky man named Toran, whose skin was a deep brown from the north wind. His fingers were short and thick but unusually nimble, and he liked to tap the table with his knuckles when he spoke. He brought a shipment of ironware and salt from the eastern city-states, as well as a shipment of "special goods"—he didn't specify what they were, but simply tapped the table three times with his knuckles.

"Lord Morion," Toran lowered his voice, "the council leaders of the eastern city-states have asked me to convey to you—they are deeply concerned about the expansion of the Northern Rift. The frost line of the rift has already advanced to the third milestone of the Old Royal Road. If this continues, the entire Northern trade route will be frozen to death by next spring."

"What does the Speaker of Parliament want me to do?"

"He wants you to step in and send a message to the forces in the north…" Toran tapped the table again with his knuckles. "Commerce cannot stop. If the trade routes in the north are cut off, it won't just be your Morion territory that suffers, but the entire eastern trade network."

Morion remained silent for a moment.

"Who are you referring to as the 'northern powers'?"

Toran's expression became subtle. He didn't answer directly, but instead drew a circle on the table with his knuckles—a map of the territories of the demigod lords on the old world map. At the north end of the circle, he lightly drew a line with his fingernail.

"You know," he said, "those beings older than us."

The words lingered in the air for a moment. Morion knew what Toran was talking about—ancient gods were active near the northern rift. But Toran wouldn't say it outright, and Morion wouldn't press for details. It was an unspoken understanding between demigods and mortals: some things are better left unsaid; saying them changes their meaning.

"I'll consider it," Morion said.

Toran nodded, stood up to take his leave. As he reached the door, he suddenly turned back:

"By the way, sir. I noticed something strange when I passed through River Valley Town on my way."

"What's up?"

"The kids in town are all singing a new song." Toran frowned. "The melody is beautiful, but I don't understand the lyrics. It's not in any language I recognize. And—" He rubbed his fingers together, as if rubbing away something invisible, "after singing it, I feel incredibly sleepy. Not the normal kind of sleepiness, but the kind where... my mind suddenly goes blank."

Morion's fingers clenched tightly under the table.

Noon. The temple.

Priest Calvin was waiting for him before the altar. He was a tall, thin middle-aged man with neatly combed gray hair. He wore the white robes of the Viranus Order, with the rune of the god of order embroidered on his chest—a perfect equilateral triangle containing an equally perfect circle.

"My lord." Calvin bowed slightly, his posture respectful but not servile. He was a priest of Veranos, but also a subject of Morion, a balance he had maintained for twenty years. "Today's morning prayer is on the chapter about 'The Rock and the Flood.' As you know, Lord Veranos taught us—"

"Order is the rock that protects against chaos." Morion finished the sentence for him, his tone flat, as if reciting a passage he had heard too many times.

Calvin smiled slightly. "You remember very well."

"I hear you recite it every day."

The ceremony began. Calvin's voice echoed through the temple walls, each word precise and powerful, like a hammer striking an anvil. The text was the God of Order's admonition to the believers: steadfast, unwavering, like a rock standing amidst a torrent. Morion knelt on the prayer mat, head bowed, his lips moving in rhythm with Calvin's voice, but his thoughts were not on the text.

He was thinking about that song.

That song that came from the East Coast. That song that his people are singing. That song that Tolan described, the one that leaves you "suddenly blank-minded."

Calvin's words told him: Order is a rock, unyielding and steadfast.

But that song was telling him—no, not telling him, but planting a thought deep in his bones—that even the rock will weather. The torrent is eternal. Not a violent torrent, but a slow, gentle, omnipresent infiltration. Like water seeping into stone, like a song seeping into a dream, like a melody you don't remember learning but can already hum.

He looked up at the statue of Veranos above the altar. The face was perfect—symmetrical, serene, without a single superfluous line. The beauty of order.

But suddenly he felt that the face was smiling.

It wasn't a friendly laugh. It was a laugh that said, "I know what you're thinking, but I don't care."

Morion closed his eyes.

By the time he emerged from the temple, it was already dark. Dusk falls quickly in the valley; before the sun had fully set, the western sky was already shrouded in a thin, grayish-purple mist. Morion walked along the stone path back to his mansion, his guards keeping a distance of three steps behind him.

He met someone on the road.

He wasn't one of his subjects. The man stood in the middle of the road, wearing a cloak of an indescribable color—in the twilight, it was a color that seemed gray, blue, or something else entirely outside the visible spectrum. His face was hidden by the hood, but Morion could sense that he was being watched.

The guards' hands were already on their sword hilts.

"No need," Morion raised his hand to stop them. He looked at the man, his heart racing but his voice steady, "Who are you?"

The person didn't answer. He simply stood there, the hem of his cloak fluttering gently in the evening breeze. Then he—or she, or it—reached out a hand.

The hand was black. Not just a darker skin tone, but a pure, absolute black, like a fragment cut from the night sky. A thin, elongated crack ran through the center of the palm, from which a faint, pulsating blue light emanated.

A deep blue. The same deep blue as the liquid seeping from his back.

Morion's breath caught in his throat for a moment.

Then the person withdrew their hand, turned, and walked into the alleyway beside the road. The corner of the cloak flashed one last time at the corner and disappeared.

"My lord!" The head guard rushed forward, but the alley was empty.

Morion stood there, looking down into the empty alley. The evening breeze carried the damp scent of the valley, and something deeper, more ancient—like the salt of the deep sea, like the ash left on a forgotten altar.

"Go back," he said.

He didn't sleep that night.

He sat in his study, a map of the Morion territory spread out before him. The Morion River meandered across the map, dividing the territory into east and west halves. The west bank was his traditional domain, where Veranos's influence stretched like an invisible net, covering every village and every field. The east bank—

Things are changing in Hedong.

He picked up his quill and marked the anomalies that had occurred in the last three months on the east side of the map: unfamiliar hymns began to be sung at the harvest festivals of three villages; amulets of unknown origin appeared in the markets of two towns , said to bring good luck, but those who wore them would begin to have strange dreams; and the children of River Valley learned a new song, the lyrics of which no one could decipher.

He then drew a line at the northern end of the map—the frost line of the fissure. A seventeen-mile expansion. Toran's third milestone.

Then he drew a circle on the east bank of the map—the place where he had stood in his dream the night before.

Three marks. Three anomalies. Were they connected? He didn't know. But he knew one thing: he stood at the center of something he couldn't fully comprehend. The priests of Veranos told him that order was the rock. The hymns of Aethro told him that even the rock could weather. Nerekara—

He stopped writing.

Nerekara. Mother of the Abyss. He had never mentioned this name in public, but he knew of her existence. Every demigod knew. She was the third force seated on the Twilight's throne, not involved in the struggle between the East and West Banks, but she watched over everything. Her messenger—

He remembered the cloaked, black-handed figure.

I remembered the blue light shining through the crack in the center of my palm.

Morion put down his quill and rubbed his temples. The candle in the study was almost burned out; the flame danced on the last bit of wax, casting his shadow on the wall, huge and distorted.

He suddenly realized something.

He didn't know when he first heard that song. Not today, not yesterday, not recently. It was much earlier. So much earlier that he couldn't trace the beginning. The song wasn't sung to him—it had grown in his memory without his knowledge, like a seed that had taken root without his notice.

He hummed the melody of the song.

It's just a short segment. A few notes.

Then he abruptly shut his mouth.

Because he felt it. The instant he hummed those notes, his divine essence—the demigod mark—vibrated. Not in pain, but in a resonance, as if something was responding to him. In a very far place. In the deepest, deepest place.

Beneath the frost in the cracks.

He walked to the window and opened it.

The night wind swept in, carrying the dampness of the valley and the faint chill of the north. There was no moon in the sky, and the stars were sparse and dim. But on the northern horizon, he saw a light that did not belong to nature—a very faint, pulsating, pale blue glow, as if something enormous was slowly breathing below the horizon.

He stared at it for a long time.

Then he closed the window, returned to his desk, picked up his quill pen, and wrote two words at the northern end of the map—the location of the crack:

Who is breathing?

The handwriting is very steady.

But his hand was trembling as he wrote.
