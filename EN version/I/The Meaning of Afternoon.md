#  The Meaning of Afternoon

The courtyard was quiet, and the sycamore leaves seemed to linger in the golden twilight of the divine realm.

Lin Jing sat on the stone steps with her back against the door frame, an open book on her lap, but her gaze was not on the pages.

The surface of the small stone pool in the courtyard reflected the light, like a piece of silver that had been polished thin. When the wind stopped, even those fragments of light froze, and the whole world became a painting that hadn't quite dried yet, but had been laid out to dry on an easel.

There was a warm, fluffy little thing at his feet, curled up like a ball of wet cotton. That was Gamma, less than three days old. Most of the time she slept, occasionally waking up to look around blankly with her round eyes still veiled in blue, then yawning, revealing a few translucent baby teeth, before burying them back into her tail.

"It's a good thing you can sleep." Lin Jing looked down at it.

Gamma's ear tips twitched, but its eyes remained closed. Its ears were light gray with a ring of downy fur around the edge, as if licked by frost. Lin Jing reached out and rubbed the ears with the back of his fingers; the touch was almost imperceptible. Gamma made a gurgling sound in its throat and nuzzled its head into his palm.

Lin Jing withdrew her hand and placed it on her knee.

A corner of the book was lifted by the wind and fell back down. He glimpsed that the page contained architectural drawings from the ancient times of the divine kingdom. He pressed his thumb against the spine, but showed no intention of looking at it again.

At the other end of the stone steps stood several petunias, their vines cascading down from the rim of their pots, bearing a single, half-withered purple flower. He stared at that flower for a long time.

But the flower remained still. It simply withered there, lingering at the very end of its life, neither completely faded nor rejuvenated.

Lin Jing suddenly felt a throbbing in his temple. That familiar discomfort surged up—like something pounding around in his chest, unable to find a way out, making his bones ache slightly. He clenched his fist, his nails digging into his palm, the brief stinging pain causing the restlessness to recede half a step.

"Repair," he said softly, more to himself.

He sat cross-legged, placed his hands in front of his lower abdomen, palms facing upwards. His breathing became deep, and the rise and fall of his abdomen slowly lengthened—inhale, pause, exhale, pause again. As the Qi sank through his meridians, the restlessness was gradually calmed down; though not extinguished, it at least stopped churning violently.

He remained in that position for a long time. Gamma woke up sometime earlier, wobbled over to his crossed legs, and touched his wrist with her cool, wet nose. Lin Jing didn't open her eyes. Gamma touched her twice more, and finding no response, turned around, her four short legs awkwardly supporting her round body, and took two steps towards the stone pool. Then she tripped over a protruding pebble and rolled into a ball of fluff.

Lin Jing's eyelids twitched, but she still didn't open her eyes.

The Qi in his meridians had completed a full cycle. He finished his practice, his shoulders relaxed, and the violent energy that had been lurking in his bones resurfaced—a little weaker than before, sticky and tingling.

Gamma was lying on the edge of the pool, tentatively reaching out its paws to probe the water's surface. Touching the coolness, it withdrew them, shook off its wet paw tips, and then looked up at Lin Jing. Those blue-tinted eyes knew nothing.

"You're in the best position right now," Lin Jing said, her voice a little hoarse. "Don't think about anything."

Gamma tilted her head.

"When you grow a little older and your psychic abilities awaken—" he paused, "you'll know what I'm talking about. Then you probably won't want to stay at my feet anymore."

Gamma, of course, couldn't understand. It simply rolled over, exposing its pinkish-white belly to the sun, curled its four paws around its chest, and fell asleep again.

Lin Jing looked at it. The little creature was breathing evenly, its heartbeat quick and light, like a string of tiny drumbeats transmitted through its fur.

He stood up. Startled, Gamma rolled over, groggily opened one eye, and seeing that it was him moving around, closed it again with relief.

Lin Jing suddenly remembered a long time ago—when he had just been adopted by the academy. At that time, he thought life was a series of steps that kept climbing upwards, with a newer step after each one. Later, when he reached the top of the steps, he found that it was flat ground. So flat that he could see straight through it, and there was no better scenery at the edge, only the wind blowing back and forth.

He lowered his head, looking at his hands. His fingers were distinct, and his palms bore thin calluses from years of holding a pen and forming hand seals. These hands could draw precise resonance symbols, channel spiritual energy into designated acupoints, and perform delicate operations that would earn the praise of the academy's teachers. But at this moment, they rested empty on his knees, his fingertips slightly curled, like seashells stranded on a beach, unable to reach the sea even when opened.

Gamma crawled over and rested its head on his foot, the warm little weight pressing down on his toes. It was asleep again. Lin Jing didn't move. He was afraid that if he moved his foot, that warm thing would wake up, and wake up to look at him with those eyes that knew nothing. He wasn't afraid of being looked at—he had been looked at for so many years, he was used to it—what he was afraid of was those eyes that were too clean, so clean that they could reflect things that he himself didn't want to look at closely.

He didn't know how long he had been sitting there. The sun began to set, and the shadows of the sycamore trees stretched long up the stone steps, inch by inch covering his clothes. Gamma's breathing was even and long, and a gecko silently crawled across the courtyard wall. Everything was running its usual course, all things in the world were in their proper places, only he sat there, his limbs all intact yet feeling as if a piece of him was missing.

The wind finally stirred, blowing across the surface of the stone pool, crumpling and smoothing the silver piece. He gazed at the shimmering fragments of light, his lips moving almost inaudibly:

"What is the meaning of my life?"

The wind didn't answer, nor did Gamma. He had asked himself this question many times. After so many times, the question was no longer sharp; like a thin root beneath the skin, it no longer hurt, but only subtly tugged at every inch of the blood vessels.

He tugged at the corners of his mouth, revealing a very faint smile that vanished in an instant.

"Never mind," he said softly.

Gamma suddenly rolled over, belly up, and unconsciously pawed at the air with its four paws. Its paws were too short, and after pawing for a long time, it couldn't reach anything. Finally, it put its paws down in frustration, tilted its head, and let out a soft whimper.

Lin Jing looked down at it, reached out, and gently placed his index finger in Gamma's palm. The little creature immediately wrapped its two front paws around his finger, holding it tightly, and then buried its face contentedly between his fingers.

In that instant, the warmth from his fingertips was subtle yet tangible. He felt Gamma's heartbeat, fast and light, traveling through the thin layer of skin to his fingertips, fluttering like a young sprout struggling in the soil.

He withdrew his hand, stood up, and walked into the house. He prepared dinner, prepared for his evening study, prepared to lie down and then open his eyes to greet yet another identical dawn. Behind him, Gamma rolled over, curled itself back into a ball of fur, and continued its dream that no one else could understand.

The last ray of light slid down the courtyard wall, and dusk crept in.
During night meditation, moonlight slanted in through the window, carving a cold, white rectangle on the floor tiles in front of the meditation cushion. Lin Jing sat on the edge of that rectangle, legs crossed, palms up on her knees. The spiritual energy in her meridians had already circulated for three cycles, becoming increasingly sluggish, like a riverbed clogged with too much silt, causing the water to overflow halfway and soak the banks.

A fine layer of sweat appeared on his forehead. His dantian was burning hot, and there was a sore, aching sensation deep in his lower abdomen, as if it had been repeatedly rubbed. According to the guidance in the "Basic Spiritual Communication Method," he was supposed to begin the fifth cycle of spiritual energy to break through the third acupoint of the Ren meridian. He should have completed this step three days ago, but every time his spiritual energy gathered at that point, it would be bounced back, like a locked door.

Door.

He opened his eyes, staring at the patch of moonlight on the floor tiles, and an image inexplicably surfaced in his mind—a deep well, with moss hanging down from the opening, the bottom so dark that nothing could be seen, only damp, cold steam rising upwards. He had never seen such a strange deep well in reality.

He closed his eyes again.

Halfway through the fourth day, his spiritual energy suddenly veered off course, rushing up a side meridian it shouldn't have, causing a sudden numbness below his shoulder blade. He groaned and quickly cut off the flow, letting the erratic energy dissipate on its own. Cold sweat trickled down the back of his neck and into his collar, feeling both cool and itchy.

Beside the futon was a celadon dish containing three spiritual fruits, which were distributed by the academy's canteen at lunchtime. At this moment, their skins were covered with fine wrinkles, like three faces that were about to grow old.

He stared at the three fruits for a moment, then suddenly reached out and picked one up, holding it between his fingers. The flesh was soft and mushy, as if it would crumble at the slightest pressure. He didn't crush it, but simply held it, feeling a cool, withering life force emanating from his fingertips.

"It hasn't gone over yet," he said softly.

There was no one else in the room, so the words simply splashed across the four walls and then dissipated. Gamma was fast asleep, curled up on the soft cushion in the corner, her ears twitching occasionally.

Lin Jing placed the spirit fruit back into the dish. The three fruits huddled together, all wilted, like three travelers sitting side by side but none of them speaking.

He resumed circulating his energy. This time, he was more careful, his spiritual power coalescing into thin threads, slowly probing along the walls of his meridians, testing the waters step by step. The blockage at the third acupoint felt heavier than before, like a wall wrapped in damp cotton. He gritted his teeth and pushed harder, his temples throbbing from the effort. The tips of the spiritual power threads were squeezed and deformed, squeezed again, deformed again. The wall wrapped in cotton remained unmoved, while his spiritual meridians began to emit a faint hum—a sign of overload.

He suddenly stopped exerting force.

He leaned back, his head hitting the wall with a dull thud. Gamma was startled awake, whimpered twice, scrambled up from the cushion, and staggered across half the room, nudging his calf with his head.

"It's alright." Lin Jing reached out and stroked its head. Gamma licked his finger and climbed back onto the cushion.

He leaned against the wall, his heart pounding rapidly in his chest. The moonlight outside the window had shifted at some point, the white rectangle sliding to the left side of the futon, its edge dividing his shadow into light and shadow. He looked down at his outstretched palm—his fingertips would glow faintly when his spiritual energy was abundant, but now that light had completely faded, leaving only a warm, weary crimson hue in his palm.

He suddenly felt tired, but the tiredness had nothing to do with the setback in his spiritual veins. It was as if a string that had been deeply embedded had been plucked, and a buzzing sound spread from the soles of his feet all the way to the top of his head.

Three voices were playing in my mind at the same time, overlapping and out of place, like an old record needle stuck on the same scratch, turning over and over again.

"Jing'er is very talented. The academy teachers say she has a good chance of becoming a spiritual cultivator in three years. Three years! Our family is going to have a spiritual cultivator!" That was her mother's voice. As she spoke, she was squatting in front of the stove adding firewood. Her profile was reflected in the firelight, and the lines at the corners of her eyes were smoothed out, like a piece of paper that had been moistened with water.

"Look at Old Liu's son next door. He just got into the outer city school last year and he brags about it to everyone he meets. If our Jing'er becomes a spiritual cultivator—" The father didn't finish his sentence. He just downed the half-cup of cheap wine in one gulp. His Adam's apple bobbed up and down a lot. When he laughed, his mouth stretched halfway before closing up again, as if he was too embarrassed to show his happiness too obviously.

"Brother, will you be able to fly in the future? Will you be able to use magic to make candy?" His little sister was only six years old at the time, with a missing front tooth, and she tugged at his sleeve, looking up at him and asking. He said he didn't know, so his sister stomped her foot and said, "Then you'd better learn quickly!"

He pressed his back even harder against the wall. He didn't know when it started, but those voices no longer brought him warmth. They had become invisible ropes, drawn from his distant home, tightly bound to his wrists, ankles, and neck. Whenever he slacked off, hesitated, or wanted to stop and catch his breath, the threads would tighten, a silent pull coming from the other end—forward, forward, spiritualist, spiritualist. They didn't hurt, but they bound him tightly, forcing him to measure the depth of each breath.

He wasn't afraid of hardship. He could endure sitting meditation, reciting runes, and repeatedly trying different techniques to traverse a spiritual vein—how many breaths did it take? What he feared was that one day, the taut rope would break on its own.

The spiritual man knew what his family wanted. They all wanted a better life, and he understood that longing. He had seen his mother's chapped fingers in winter, his father arguing heatedly with a vegetable vendor at the market over a single penny, and other girls' sisters picking up discarded scraps of cloth, washing them, and sewing them into purses. Those images were etched into his mind—a urging, and also a shackle.

He became the rope that pulled his family out of the mire of life, but the rope itself didn't know what was tied to its end.

If a spiritual cultivator achieves success in their cultivation, their lifespan will be extended, they can receive a salary from the academy, and they can enter and leave the inner city of the divine realm. Then comes higher cultivation levels, longer lifespans, more salary, and larger residences. And then...

He dared not think about that "then." Because every time he thought about it, the emptiness in his chest expanded, like a well that was dug deeper and deeper, the darkness at the bottom of the well growing thicker and thicker.

He thought of that well again. Moss, damp, cold stone walls, bottomless darkness. He wasn't sure why he always saw it at this moment—perhaps because every time he strained to dig downwards, he was always faced with something that refused to see the light. That thing didn't roar or grovel; it simply remained silently black. Like a well that had swallowed too many lives, all the cries of those who fell in were absorbed by the well walls, leaving only a heavy, deathly silence.

He had heard from his academy instructors that in ancient times, humans would throw spirit beasts that had reached the end of their lifespan into deep wells as a ritual to return them to heaven and earth.
Those wells were later sealed because the accumulated resentment was too heavy and would breed evil spirits.
But the mentor also said that there is an even more ancient well, in which not resentment is deposited, but the complete memories of living beings—everything they once lived, loved, and struggled for. That kind of well is not called an evil well, but an "Echo Abyss".
It is said that if you stand at the mouth of a well and shout a sentence, you will hear hundreds of different responses from below, all of which are the last voices left in the world by those who drowned themselves in the well.

He had never seen the Echo Abyss. But every time he closed his eyes to cultivate, and failed to break through his acupoints, the darkness he felt seemed to be alive—there were many people and many creatures inside. They didn't attack him; they just huddled together, looking at him with moist eyes, their gazes filled with the same weariness.

Are you also searching for meaning? He sometimes wanted to ask that question, but he never actually did.

Gamma woke up again. This time, she didn't crawl over. Instead, she propped herself up on the cushion, tilting her head to look at him. Her blue-tinted eyes shone faintly in the darkness, a clear, untainted light. She was simply awake, feeling that her master needed to be looked at, so she looked.

Lin Jing met its gaze.

The restlessness and rage that had churned in his chest all night suddenly paused, like boiling water being poured with a ladle of cold water, momentarily still. He looked at Gamma's round, plump silhouette, at its belly rising and falling gently in the moonlight, and suddenly thought: If only I were a spirit beast. No need for spiritual awakening, no need to break through acupoints, no need to be anyone's rope. Just wake up, eat, look at your master, and sleep again when you've had your fill. I wouldn't even know the word "meaning."

Then he gave a soft "tsk" and shook his head self-deprecatingly.

If it really is a spirit beast, I'm afraid I won't be happy about it.

He straightened his legs and placed his hands together in front of his dantian. The fatigue in his spiritual meridians still weighed heavily on him, like lead seeping into his bones. But he still closed his eyes, concentrated, and drew out the remaining spiritual energy bit by bit, gathering it towards the acupoint that had been blocked for three days.

"Try again," he said.

Gamma lay back on the cushion, making gurgling sounds in his throat. The moonlight finally slid past his knees and fell on the empty floor behind the futon. The room was quiet, so quiet that only his increasingly long breaths and the faint echoes from behind the wall, which did not belong to any living thing, could be heard.
