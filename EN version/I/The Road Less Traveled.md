#  The Road Less Traveled

As the morning light fell on the white stone pillars, Lin Jing was walking through the inner courtyard of the monks' academy. Pale golden light filtered through the stained-glass windows on either side of the corridor, casting a warm glow on his uniform badge. He paused when he saw the figure ahead—reddish-brown curly hair cascading from the edge of the hood, dew clinging to the hem of his robe, as if he had run all the way there.

"You're here so early too." She turned around, her eyes crinkling with a smile. It was the young lady who had helped him pick up the scattered books at the entrance when he was admitted to the hospital. Lin Jing remembered her name, but now he suddenly realized he didn't know how to address her. Her name was Zhou Jin, the only daughter of the Zhou family in the Upper District.

"I dare not be late for the morning lessons at Tian Shu Pavilion," Lin Jing said. He noticed a very small family crest pinned to her collar, silver and thinly polished, like a leaf that could be blown away by the wind at any moment. She reached out and brushed her hair aside, the movement natural, but her hand curled slightly when it touched the family crest.

"My father is coming today," Zhou Jin suddenly said, her tone as soft as if she were talking about someone else, "and he's bringing a young man surnamed Meng. I heard he has a high spiritual rank and holds a position in the Guardian Division. His mother and my mother used to be close friends."

Lin Jing didn't reply. A morning breeze blew through the courtyard, and a few petals from the wisteria under the eaves rustled down, landing on her hood. She didn't brush them away, but simply gazed at the petals, her smile gradually fading.

"Who do you think they'll make me marry?" she said, as if asking herself.

Lin Jing stood there, his palms slightly sweaty. He should say something, but he found himself speechless. Most girls in the Upper City were like this; if they wanted their crushes to be accepted by their parents, they had to come from a prominent family and be exceptionally capable. Appearance wasn't really important—because of spiritual cultivation, appearance reflected the heart, and most spiritual cultivators were handsome. Zhou Jin was naturally beautiful, but everyone in the academy was beautiful, a beauty like a thin layer of glaze covering something hard.

"I'm going to Tian Shu Pavilion." Zhou Jin's voice broke his silence. She smiled again and walked briskly in another direction. Lin Jing watched her figure disappear between the pillars, her reddish-brown curly hair flashing in the light before being swallowed by the shadows.

The morning lesson was held in a quiet room. A dozen or so young people sat cross-legged on prayer cushions, with spirit lamps hanging overhead, casting a white and even light. The teacher, surnamed Gu, was over fifty years old, with a kind face and a reassuringly gentle demeanor when he spoke. Today he was lecturing on the spiritual energy guidance during the Foundation Establishment stage. He paused midway through his lecture, his gaze sweeping over the group.

"The greatest taboo in cultivation is greed for quick results and reckless advancement," Master Gu's voice was not loud, but every word was clear. "Spiritual veins are like river channels; raging torrents will only breach the banks. Steady progress is the right path."

Lin Jing sat in the third row. He gazed at Professor Gu's hands resting on his knees—thin yet steady hands, with thin calluses on his knuckles, the result of years of holding a writing brush. He had once been close to this teacher; when he went to ask him questions after class, Professor Gu would invite him to stay for a cup of warm tea and recount some old stories from the academy. At those times, he felt that there was light in this quiet room.

Until three months ago, the Guardian Division came to the academy to recruit high-ranking cultivators for the "Outskirts Purge." Master Gu, sitting at his desk reviewing spiritual texts, didn't even look up, only saying, "I'm too old." Later, Lin Jing met him in the corridor and tentatively asked about the purge. Master Gu looked past his shoulder into the distance and said, "I don't know."

Lin Jing never spoke to him again after that.

Master Gu was still lecturing, his voice steady, occasionally raising his hand to draw a diagram of the flow of spiritual energy in the air, pale blue light flickering at his fingertips. The students around him lowered their eyes, their posture upright, each face impeccably handsome, yet there was an air of polite distance within that beauty. Lin Jing suddenly remembered the suppressed sobs he had heard in the bathhouse last night, not knowing who it was or why. But he knew that asking was pointless, just as he knew that Master Gu actually knew everything.

Self-preservation is the norm. Indifference is the norm.

After morning classes, Lin Jing didn't return to his dormitory. Instead, he passed through the side gate of the academy and entered the inner city of the divine kingdom. The academy's uniform badges were conspicuous in the sunlight, and the patrolling guards nodded slightly upon seeing him without questioning him. The divine kingdom was vast, with the inner city streets paved with bluish-gray flags. Shops on both sides displayed various spirit flags, and the aroma of roasted spirits wafted from the teahouses. Everything was well-organized and orderly.

He walked for about 15 minutes and then turned into a relatively quiet alley where he saw the man.

A young woman, dressed in a plain gray cloth robe, stood under an old locust tree at the end of the alley. A hemp rope was tied around her waist, the other end looped around a horizontal branch—the rope was rough, leaving a red mark on her slender, white neck. She tilted her head back, her face expressionless, staring only at the dangling rope loop. A vendor selling sweet cakes pushed his cart past the alley entrance, the tinkling of his brass bells growing louder and then fading away. She didn't move.

Lin Jing stopped. He recognized her as the girl who sold talismans on East Street in the inner city; she would always give him a couple extra "for self-defense" whenever he went. She had a shallow dimple on her right cheek when she smiled.

She had her back to him at that moment, so her dimples were no longer visible.

He opened his mouth, wanting to call her name, but his throat felt blocked. He knew it would be useless. Every day in the divine realm, there were people like this, seeking their end in various ways. Corpses floated on the western moat , someone jumped from the southern bell tower, and ropes hung in the sacred mulberry grove in the northern suburbs. After they died, they would enter the sacred fire, becoming fuel, leaving not even ashes. Their spirits were drained, returning to that eternally burning white tower.

Legend has it that suffering souls have a chance to be transformed into Faceless Ones.

Lin Jing had seen a faceless person in his hometown when he was a child. It was an autumn night, and he was squatting by the courtyard wall, crying, because the neighbor's children had mocked his weak spiritual power. Suddenly, a shadow appeared beside him, wearing a mask that was neither clearly crying nor laughing, revealing only a pair of eyes. Those eyes flickered with an eerie fire, but it wasn't scorching; it just shone quietly. The faceless person squatted down beside him, quietly listening to him sob as he poured out all his grievances, without ever trying to comfort or correct him. Finally, the faceless person stood up, his voice, through the mask, like wind whistling through withered branches, leaving behind a single sentence before departing.

Lin Jing didn't understand that sentence at the time, but she gradually came to understand it later.

"When a world is riddled with holes and full of ruins, don't expect one person to end it all."

He stood in the alley, the leaves of the old locust tree rustling. The girl selling talismans still looked up, the hemp rope winding, unwinding, and winding again between her fingers. Lin Jing looked down and saw the dust from her morning prayers clinging to the tips of her boots, the pale blue powder scattered in a small patch on the stone slab.

In his youth, he dreamed of everyone shedding their masks of hypocrisy and living in peace. Under the starry sky of his hometown, he believed that with enough effort and sincerity, the cracks could be gradually mended. Later, he entered the academy, met Master Gu, Zhou Jin, the silent guards patrolling the city walls, and the ever-present flames of the Sacred Flame Tower. He gradually understood that individual strength is limited. Like a drop of water falling into boiling oil, no matter how loud the sound, it's only for a moment.

But there's one thing he hasn't fully figured out yet.

He turned around and slowly walked back the way he came. As he reached the alleyway, he glanced back one last time. The girl had let go of the rope, curled up against a tree trunk, her shoulders trembling slightly. She was alive. She was crying. Lin Jing looked away and continued walking.

The world didn't change because of his presence, but the girl is still alive. Tomorrow, perhaps she'll still be standing behind the talisman stall on East Street, a shallow dimple appearing on her right cheek when she smiles. The day after tomorrow, who knows? But today, she's still alive.

Lin Jing walked along the gray stone path, past the jingling candy carts, past the elegant, expressionless, and impeccably dressed monks. The divine kingdom was vast, the sunlight bright, and the Sacred Fire Tower burned silently in the distance. He recalled the lines on the faceless man's mask, indistinguishable between tears and laughter, and those eyes flickering with ghostly flames. The faceless man had heard all his thoughts, yet changed nothing.

But he remembered that when the last shadow stood up, Ghostfire jumped slightly, as if moved by some extremely restrained emotion.

Individual strength is indeed limited. But the world hasn't collapsed hysterically, probably because some souls are shining. Lin Jing reached into his sleeve and touched the extra "self-defense" talisman the girl selling talismans had given him; the corner of the paper was already frayed.

He clenched the talisman for a moment, then released it and continued walking forward.
