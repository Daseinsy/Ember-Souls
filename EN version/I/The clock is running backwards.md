#  The clock is running backwards

The afternoon sun beat down relentlessly, and there were no clouds in the sky above the Kingdom of God.

The laurel trees lining the corridor of the college's mission office were neatly trimmed. The administrator pushed a piece of parchment with gold-embossed edges in front of Lin Jing and pressed his fingertip on the words "Aubrey".

“Adele Aubrey, sixteen years old, the Earl’s only daughter.” He read from the script, “Three months ago, she exhibited unusual behavior at the Spring Banquet at the Royal Theatre, currently displaying persistent cognitive dissonance, self-harm tendencies, and aggressive substance projection—”

"Aggressive material projection?" Lin Jing asked in surprise.

“Yes. To be precise, it was the complete afternoon tea porcelain set, twenty-seven pieces in total, which was thrown away over nine days. The Earl’s mansion specifically noted in its report that on the last day, she threw away a sugar bowl, which hit the roof of a viscountess’s carriage that was passing by.” The caretaker paused, “The lady’s dog was frightened.”

Someone in the back row gasped very softly.

"The academy's assessment is 'psychological resonance deviation.' The first three interventions were ineffective, so we need you to try. The people from the Earl's mansion are waiting for you at the west gate; they're wearing blue backgrounds and silver irises. The other two trips are to the cemetery and the sanatorium, so don't go to the wrong place."

Lin Jing thanked him, folded the parchment, put it in the inner pocket of her robe, and turned to walk out the door.

Sure enough, three carriages were parked at the west gate. The one on the right, a blue carriage with silver irises, was quietly parked in the shade of the plane trees. The driver stood ramrod straight, his white gloves spotless. He opened the carriage door for Lin Jing, his palm facing upwards above the door frame: "Mr. Lin, the Earl's mansion is at No. 7 Parma Street, about forty minutes away. Would you like some tea or water?"

No. 7 Parma Street was older than the surrounding houses. Withered vines covered the stone walls, their dry, brown stems clinging to the greyish-white surface. The Aubrey crest on the iron gate—a raven carrying a key—had been worn smooth by the wind and rain.

The butler, around sixty years old, was waiting at the door. His back was still straight, his silver-gray hair was perfectly styled, and his sharp eyes calmly scanned the guest from head to toe before returning to his face.

The hall was dimly lit, with two layers of curtains hanging in the windows, and a family portrait above the fireplace, the gold leaf faded badly from the figures' heads.

The study door was ajar. Before she could even raise her hand, the sound of a cane tapping the floor came from inside—tap, tap, tap—with long intervals.

"Please come in." The door opened silently and automatically, revealing Count Aubrey by the window. He wore a dark gray coat, one button undone at the collar, but still maintained an air of dignified composure. He leaned on his cane, his hands resting on the top, his knuckles white. His face looked older than in the portrait.

Dark flames flickered in the fireplace beside him, and strange objects were displayed in the niches on either side: the cross-section of a conch shell was cut open, revealing the iridescence of mother-of-pearl; the root of some kind of plant was soaking in a glass jar, the liquid already murky; and there was a small piece that looked like a fossil, with spiral patterns on it.

“Miss Lin,” the Earl said without turning around, his gaze fixed on the objects, “do you see these?”

"I saw it."

“They were all collected by Adele. Seashells, tree roots, stones. She said there were sounds in these things—the wind from the past lived in the seashells, and the sound of water from underground was locked in the tree roots. I told her they were useless things, but she wouldn’t listen.”

Lin Jing stopped and looked for two seconds.

“In the first three sessions, one person used guided meditation, another used aromatherapy and music therapy, and yet another attempted to discipline her behavior.”

"How was the result?"

The Count chuckled. "The first time, she threw someone's incense burner out the window. The second time, she recited the meditation guide backwards until the therapist started to question his existence. The third time, the disciplinarian stayed in Adele's room for less than half an hour, and when he came out, his tie was missing—the butler later found it on the chandelier."

Lin Jing didn't laugh. "Your daughter said 'the clock is running backwards,' was that at the spring banquet?"

The Count's lips twitched slightly. "The Royal Theatre. Spring Banquet, Act III, *Orfeo*. During intermission, Adele suddenly stood up, standing very quietly. Everyone around assumed she was going to give a speech—she sometimes did. But she simply said: 'Listen, the clocks are going backwards.'"

Who was sitting next to her at the time?

The count was silent for a few seconds. “Her mother and I sat at the same table, but three seats apart. To her right was the son of a viscount, and to her left was an empty seat—originally, it was reserved for a friend she had known since childhood, a girl named Renault. But the Renault family didn’t come that day.”

"Why?"

"Miss Renault got engaged two months ago to a hereditary baron in the North. She moved to the North last month."

Lin Jing nodded and didn't ask any further questions.

A series of clattering sounds came from the other end of the hall, silverware hitting porcelain. The Earl's brow twitched. "Frank is having tea. He doesn't like visitors at this time; he gets a little grumpy."

Can I see him?

The count glanced into the distance: "If the door is open when you pass by, he will probably see you first."

The door to the east hall was indeed half-open. A boy was curled up in a dark green velvet sofa, with untouched almond cookies and a half-splashed cup of black tea on the low table in front of him. He was poking at the bottom of the cup with a silver spoon, the scraping sound delicate and persistent. His face, though fourteen years old, still had a somewhat empty look, but when he looked up, there was a prematurely mature detachment in his expression.

“You can’t handle her,” Frank said, spoon still in hand. “I don’t understand a word she says. It wasn’t like this before. She used to read me sailing stories, the story of the boy who drifted for seven days and seven nights inside a whale, and then she’d ask me, ‘Frank, if it were you, would you be scared or curious?’”

"What did you say?" Lin Jing asked.

Frank stopped eating. “I said I was scared. She laughed and said, ‘Then you have to practice your curiosity.’” He placed the spoon on the edge of the plate with a clinking sound, then turned his face toward the north-facing window. “After that, she only talked to the wall, and even when I stood next to her, she wouldn’t look at me.”

As the housekeeper escorted Lin Jing through the short corridor, she asked Adele about her childhood.

“When Miss Adele was five years old,” the butler said, “her mother bought her a picture book with an illustration of a town inside a whale’s belly. She looked at the picture for three whole days, and then told me that she dreamed she lived inside it.”

“You remember it very clearly.”

“I worked as Aubrey’s housekeeper for 31 years, and there are some things that need to be kept more than just an account book.”

He stopped in front of the stormy sailboat painting. The sails in the painting were billowing in the wind, there were tiny white bubbles on the crests of the waves, the dark clouds hung low, but the tiny figure at the bow stood straight.

“Miss Adele said the painting is wrong. She said no one would stand so straight in a storm, and the painter should have crouched down to paint it if he were that sailor.” He finished speaking and continued walking forward, with Lin Jing following behind him.

Adele's door was white. The crayon stars and moons on it had faded a bit, and the edges were rubbed in several places. A very faint humming came from under the door, the tune elusive and uncertain.

The butler pushed open the door and took a step back.

Lin Jing stepped across the threshold. The broken porcelain shards scattered on the floor were arranged in an irregular spiral according to their shades of color, swirling from the doorway into the depths of the room. The curtains were torn into knotted strips, the knots interlocking to form a net suspended in mid-air.

Adele sat in the center of the net. She was curled up in the corner, leaning against the wall, her legs bent, her chin resting on her knees. Her golden hair was a tangled mess, tangled with dry grass stems and a small piece of dried osmanthus.

When she looked up at Lin Jing, her eyes sent a chill down Lin Jing's spine—they were so clear, like the water at the very top of a mountain stream, chillingly cold, yet so transparent that you could see right to the bottom. There was no chaos in them, no unfocused or evasive pupils often seen in the eyes of a madman, only an overly sharp clarity.

“You’ve come,” Adele said, her humming ceasing. “I’ve been waiting for you for so long.”

Lin Jing squatted down three steps away from her. She didn't rush to speak, but followed Adele's gaze to look around—at first glance it was chaotic, but after staring for a while, a hidden order slowly emerged.

"What are you arranging?" Lin Jing asked, pointing to the porcelain shards on the ground.

Adele tilted her head, a strand of golden hair entwined with a blade of grass sliding down her cheek: "The clock face. The floor is round, and it took me nine steps to get here from the doorway; nine steps make a semicircle. The broken porcelain is the scale, but there's no needle. The needle is elsewhere."

Where is the needle?

Adele raised her hand, wrapped in a white bandage, and pointed to her temple: “Walk here. Walk one circle every day, backwards. From twelve to eleven to ten, yesterday on the left, tomorrow on the bottom.” She suddenly smiled, her lips chapped, but the smile was genuine. “Do you know why everyone thinks I’m crazy? Because they only look forward, no one is willing to look back and count the marks they’ve passed.”

Lin Jing felt the bell in her chest cavity vibrate.

“You heard the bells.” Adele suddenly leaned closer, her clear eyes almost touching Lin Jing’s. She smelled of dried osmanthus and ink. “You have a sound. It’s far away, but it keeps ringing. It’s in the same direction as my bells.”

Lin Jing's fingertips began to go numb. Her healing instincts told her she should keep her distance, but she just squatted there, watching the light in Adele's eyes gradually gather.

"What's your name?" Adele asked.

"Lin Jing."

"Lin Jing," Adele murmured the name twice, "There is stillness in the forest. You are quiet inside, but the bell is ringing. Can you hear it?"

Lin Jing closed her eyes.

She heard it.

When Lin Jing opened her eyes, her back was against the wall, the cool floor seeping into her robe. She found herself sitting on the floor of Adele's room, her knees curled up, in almost the exact same position as the girl in the corner. Adele was asleep. Her head was tilted to one side, her disheveled blonde hair covering half her face, and her breathing was steady.

The broken porcelain shards beside me are arranged in a spiral, gradually deepening from light blue to dark blue, with a curved gold line drawn on a small piece of broken pottery in the center.

Lin Jing leaned against the wall and stood up. There were very light footsteps in the corridor outside the door, but they stopped at the door without coming in.

"Miss is asleep."

"She's asleep," Lin Jing said.

The butler nodded, stepped aside, and cleared the corridor. Lin Jing gently closed the door behind her as she left.

“Miss Lin,” he followed half a step behind her down the stairs, “Miss Lin, she…”

“She will be alright,” Lin Jing said, “but she won’t become normal again.”

The butler was silent for a moment. As he reached the corner on the second floor, he said, "In the thirty years I've lived with the Aubrey family, I've seen Miss Aubrey smile in many different ways." He paused, "But the kind of smile she had in her dream just now, I've never seen before."

Which one?

"It was like someone finally caught up with her."

Lin Jing didn't answer. Reaching the first floor, the Earl was still standing before the fireplace, leaning on his cane, facing the unlit firewood in the hearth.

“Miss Lin.” He didn’t turn around.

"Your Excellency."

“She’s asleep,” the count nodded. “She hasn’t been quiet before sunset in three months. Today…”

He didn't finish speaking.

"Can I ask you something?" Lin Jing said.

"ask."

How did Adele find out that Miss Renault had moved to the North?

"On the morning of the Spring Banquet, the Renault family sent a letter saying that they were temporarily unwell and would be absent from the banquet. Adele had grown up with her since childhood. She opened the letter, read it without saying anything, folded it up, and put it in her desk drawer. That afternoon, she asked me to find her the 'Navigation Atlas'—the one from when she was a child—and turned to the page about whales, looking at it for a long time."

"Were you next to her at the time?"

“I was in my study dealing with mail.” The Earl paused. “I should have gone in that afternoon. But I didn’t. She came down at dinner time, and everything was normal. The next day, when I took her to the theater, she stood up and pushed away that glass of wine, and I knew it was all over.”

Lin Jing looked into his eyes and said nothing.

She walked out, the door to the east hall still ajar as she passed. Frank stood in the doorway, still clutching the silver spoon, its surface stained with dried tea.

"Is she asleep?"

"She's asleep."

Frank's lips moved, his grey-blue eyes swept across her face, finally settling on her eyes: "What did you do to her?"

"I listened to the bells with her for a while."

"What bell is that?"

"I heard your sister say that time is going backwards."

Frank stared at her for several seconds. “She used to ask me if I could hear,” he said, his voice low and menacing. “I said I couldn’t. She’d say, ‘Really? Then maybe only some people can hear.’ After that, she stopped asking me.”

Lin Jing squatted down to meet his gaze. "Just because you can't hear it doesn't mean it doesn't exist. Your sister isn't crazy. She just heard things that others couldn't, and she thought she was the only one in the world who could hear them. Now she knows that's not true."

Frank's eyelashes fluttered slightly: "I'm going to college next year. I'll apply to study resonance theory and see if I can learn to listen."

Lin Jing stood up. Frank suddenly reached out and tugged at the cuff of her robe, then quickly let go.

Will you come again tomorrow?

“Not tomorrow,” she saw the light in his eyes dim for a moment, “but the day after tomorrow. When she wakes up, she needs someone to tell her that what happened yesterday was real, not a dream.”

Frank nodded, gripping his spoon.

The housekeeper escorted her to the door, and it was already completely dark when the iron gate opened.

“Miss Lin,” the butler said as she stepped out the door, “when Miss Adele was five years old, she dreamed of a town inside a whale’s belly. The first thing she said to me the next morning was, ‘Grandpa Butler, there’s a clock in that town, but it runs very slowly. It only moved three notches all morning.’ At the time, I thought it was just childish nonsense. Today I think, perhaps she heard it back then.”

Lin Jing stood at the boundary between the streetlight and the darkness, and glanced back at him. "Tell her," Lin Jing said, "that the clock inside the whale's belly had someone keep it company for a few steps today."

The following evening, the Earl's carriage stopped again at the academy gate. This time, it wasn't the coachman who came to pick her up, but the butler himself. He stood beside the carriage, his white gloves clasped in front of him.

“Miss Lin, the first question the young lady asked after waking up was, ‘Is that person coming today?’ The Earl sent me to invite you.”

Lin Jing got into the car. Upon arriving at No. 7 Parma Street, the housekeeper led her directly to the third floor. Adele sat on the bed, not curled up in the corner, but on the edge, her legs dangling down, her toes barely touching the floor. Her hair was simply gathered behind her head; the straw and osmanthus blossoms had been removed, revealing a clean, washed face. The bandage on her right index finger had been replaced, a new white cloth neatly wrapped around it.

She saw Lin Jing come in, but just looked at her, her clear, overly bright eyes quietly resting on Lin Jing's face.

“I had a dream yesterday,” Adele said.

"What dream?"

“I’m standing in a white fog, and I have a key in my hand.” She stretched out her hand, palm open—there was nothing there, but she looked down at it, “but my key is rusty and won’t go into the lock.”

Lin Jing sat down on the edge of the bed. Only the two of them remained in the room, an old grandfather clock ticking in the corner.

Adele withdrew her hand and tilted her head to look at her: "Can you come into my dream tonight?"

Lin Jing looked at her, recalling her own tower window sill, her dangling feet, the hollow sound of the wind blowing into her robe. Behind that window was also a door, but she had never actually turned the handle.

“Yes,” she said.

Adele blinked, not out of fear, but with a serious expression. "Then you must be careful. There are things in dreams that I can't see when I'm awake; but they're there when I'm asleep." She leaned back, sinking into the pillow, her golden hair spreading out. She closed her eyes, her lips moving slightly as she hummed that off-key tune—the same rhythm that had wafted through the crack in the door yesterday.

Lin Jing took out the academy's resonance talisman and affixed it to Adele's forehead. The talisman was slightly warm. Adele closed her eyes and sank her consciousness into that soft darkness.

The initial feeling of falling lingered for a long time. The wind whistled past my ears, accompanied by the crisp sound of shattering glass. Then my feet touched the ground.

The world of dreams is tilted. The sky is an inverted indigo lake, and the clouds sink beneath her feet. She stands on a path paved with old sheet music, the notes on the staff raised like rough grains, making a rustling sound as she walks on them. In the distance, buildings are crooked and twisted—the top of a clock tower is twisted into a spiral, the hands circling the tower; the branches of several trees droop down, their tips adorned with extinguished lanterns.

Adele stood at the end of the path. She was wearing the dress from her dream—a pale blue dress studded with sequins, the hem of which was sewn with several real little scissors that jingled as she moved. Her hair was braided into a long braid that hung down to her chest, with a silver bell tied to the end.

Adele waved to her. Lin Jing quickened her pace to catch up. Things began to appear on both sides of the path: a chair leaning to one side, with withered roses stuck in its back; a mirror half-buried in sheet music, its surface shimmering with a mercury mist, unable to reflect any image; several white pillars standing in the wilderness, their surfaces covered with densely packed words, the handwriting as clumsy as a child's scribbles.

“Those are my diaries,” Adele noticed her gaze, “but in my dream they grew up on the pillars. I tried to pull them down, but I couldn’t.” As she spoke, she made a pulling motion, and the bells at the ends of her braids jingled.

Lin Jing approached a pillar. The writing was distorted by the ever-growing wood fibers, but it was still faintly legible: "Today Father said I was out of place again." "Mother never looks in my direction at the dinner table." "Frank folded my manuscript into paper boats and put them in the fountain."

She withdrew her hand, her fingertips numb.

“What did you see?” Adele leaned closer. “I can never remember everything when I wake up. It’s like looking through a damp cloth; I can feel the shape, but I can’t see the color.”

"What are you looking for?" Lin Jing asked.

Adele tilted her head. “Looking for an echo. When I was little, I would shout into a well, and it would answer me. Later, the well was filled in, so I would shout into a wall—but the echo from the wall was muffled, like being suffocated by a pillow.” She paused and turned, the silver bell at the end of her braid drawing an arc. “Then one day I discovered that if I dreamed deep enough, there was another voice waiting for me in the depths. It wasn’t my voice.” She stared into Lin Jing’s eyes. “That voice sounded a bit like yours.”

At the end of the path stood a door with a keyhole in the center. A faint light, a pre-dawn grey-blue, shone through the crack in the door, floating quietly on the ground.

Adele stopped, her fingers tracing the cracks in the door: "I can't open it. I've tried all the keys—real, fake, the ones I found in my dreams—none of them work. But I know someone is knocking on the back. Listen."

Lin Jing held her breath. A few breaths later, a very light knock came from deep inside the door—two short knocks and one long knock, exactly the same as the one she had heard downstairs last night.

“That’s not an echo,” Lin Jing said.

“No,” Adele nodded in agreement. “The echo is from outside. That—it’s from inside.” She turned to look at Lin Jing, a thin layer of tears welling up in her excessively clear eyes. “Can you help me open it? I’ve been waiting here for so long. So long that I can’t even distinguish between wakefulness and dreams during the day. They all say I’m crazy—maybe I am. But what does it matter if I’m crazy or not. The thing behind the door really exists.”

Lin Jing stared at the door, at the grey-blue light streaming through the crack. She remembered the tower window, her dangling feet, the hollow sound of wind rushing into her robe. Behind that window was another door, but she had never actually turned the handle. She reached out, and in her palm lay a brass key—the key to reality, which had somehow followed her into her dream. Rusty, yet the teeth marks fit perfectly with the outline of the keyhole.

“You gave it to me when I came,” Lin Jing said.

Adele's tears finally fell, a thin line running down her cheek. She nodded.

Lin Jing inserted the key into the lock. The rusted edge scraped against the metal groove, emitting a long, hoarse groan. She turned it—at first it was stiff, as if something was blocking it from behind the door; then with a little more force, with a soft click, the lock cylinder sprang open. The door opened automatically inward.

A light shone forth, not a blinding white, but a warm, amber-gold hue, carrying a subtle warmth. Behind the door was a corridor lined with rows of puppets dressed in formal attire and wearing masks, all facing the center. At the end of the corridor stood an old piano, its lid open, the keys pressing down by themselves, playing a slow, simple melody—a lullaby.

Adele stepped inside, with Lin Jing following behind. The puppets bowed slightly; nothing could be seen behind their masks, but their movements were perfectly synchronized.

“They’ve been waiting for me here all along,” Adele said, with a strange sense of relief in her voice. “They don’t say ‘you’re crazy.’ They just stand there and listen to me play the piano.”

She walked to the piano, sat down, and placed her fingers on the keys, matching them with the automatically moving keys to complete the missing notes in the melody.

Lin Jing stood by the door, watching Adele play the piano. She was absorbed and quiet, completely different from the girl who had curled up in the corner humming during the day—but her eyes were the same, clear, almost too bright.

"You found it," Lin Jing said softly.

Adele looked up, her fingertips clicking. "Yes, it's been knocking, trying to get me to come in." She freed one hand and pointed towards the door. "Are you going back?"

Lin Jing turned her head and glanced at the open door. Outside, there was a slanted sky, upside-down clouds, a path paved with musical scores, and crooked buildings in the distance. "I need to go back," she said.

Adele played a few more bars, then stopped, twirling over to her. She stood on tiptoe and gently placed something into Lin Jing's palm. It was the brass key. Now it was smooth and new, all the rust gone, revealing the original luster of the brass.

“Take it,” Adele said. “Yours is rusty, this one is for you.”

Lin Jing gripped the key tightly, feeling the lingering warmth of Adele's palm on the metal. "After you wake up, will you still remember this place?"

Adele laughed, her eyes curving into two crescent moons: "Through a damp cloth, you can still feel the shape."

Golden light streamed in from the depths of the corridor, warmly enveloping the ankles of the two figures. The puppets bowed in unison, their masks tilting slightly. The piano continued to play automatically, the final note lingering, fading, fading until it finally melted into the light.

Lin Jing felt the ground beneath her feet soften. Her vision blurred at the edges, the colors of the world fading. Before completely succumbing to the dream, she took one last look at Adele—the girl stood by the piano, the silver bells at the end of her braid hanging quietly, her overly bright eyes blinking, curving into a clear, serene smile. Then she woke up.

Morning light streamed in through the east window, illuminating the sheets. Adele lay beside her, breathing evenly, a lingering tremor of sleep still clinging to her eyelashes. The calming teacup on the table was empty; the clock had just struck seven.

Lin Jing sat up and opened her palm. There was nothing in her palm except a faint trace of sweat and a slightly reddish mark shaped like a brass key. She clenched her fist and held the mark in her hand.

Lin Jing stood up and walked to the door, glancing back at Adele on the bed. The girl turned over in her sleep, her golden hair tangled in the corner of the pillow, her lips slightly parted, uttering a complete murmur—"...Next time you come, bring the key."

Lin Jing gently closed the door. Sunlight streamed into the corridor, and she stepped through the dappled light as she descended the stairs, each step feeling more solid than the last.

The imprint on her palm gradually faded. But she felt she would remember the shape of that smooth, rust-free key for a long, long time.

The bell in her chest was still ringing. But a resonant aftershock, gentle yet persistent, had been added to its rhythm. She couldn't redeem herself. The bell in her heart continued to tick, its direction still different from the world's. But at least in a gray-white stone house in the inner city of the divine realm, a girl knew as she fell asleep that the voice she heard wasn't a hallucination. Someone else heard it with her.

The sound of horses' hooves echoed on the stone slabs as the carriage carried her towards the academy. A night breeze slipped in through the gaps in the curtains, carrying the lingering sweetness of dried osmanthus blossoms. Lin Jing didn't open her eyes; she simply turned her numb hand over, palm up, and placed it on her knee, slightly open, waiting for something to fall in.
