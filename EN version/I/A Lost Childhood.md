#  A Lost Childhood

The morning light crept in from the eastern mountain ridge, slanting across the windowpane and landing on Su Mo'er's closed eyelashes. Her breathing was light and long, as if floating on soft feathers.

She dreamed of her mother.

In the courtyard, Mother was unfolding a dark blue quilt and draping it over a bamboo pole. The afternoon sun, like honey, gently spread across the quilt, and the air was filled with the sweet aroma of dried chrysanthemums and red dates, making one feel lazy.

"Mo'er, come here."

The mother knelt down and opened her arms to her.

Su Mo'er jogged over, her mother's warm breath enveloping her. Her chin rested on the top of her head, softly humming that nameless tune. In the distance, a crow cawed twice in an oak tree, then fell silent.

"Mom," Su Mo'er buried her face in her mother's clothes, inhaling the reassuring scent, "What day is it today?"

The mother smiled, a gentle vibration coming from her chest: "Eight years ago today, you drove me crazy, making a ruckus from early morning until sunset."

"Do you still like me?"

"What do you think?" Her mother nuzzled her hair with the tip of her nose, then didn't say anything more, only hugged her tighter.

Su Mo'er closed her eyes, feeling as if she were immersed in a pool of warm water.

She thought, if only time could stand still at that moment.

But the sky in my dream gradually changed.

The dark blue quilt was still drying on the bamboo pole, but the autumn sun gradually dimmed, first spreading a thin layer of dusky yellow from the edges, then revealing a glaring red within that yellow, thick like melting blood, staining half the sky.

The sounds of iron hooves and mournful cries echoed in the distance, deep and muffled like thunder rolling in from the horizon.

"Go into the cellar." The mother lowered her voice, but it trembled. "Remember, no matter what you hear, you must not make a sound. As long as there is any sound of people moving around, you cannot come out."

Su Mo'er felt as if she were being pushed into the house by an invisible force. She turned around, but her mother's face began to blur; her lips moved, but no sound could be heard. That always smiling face became like a piece of paper soaked in water, wrinkling and peeling away from the eyebrows, crumbling into pieces.

Su Mo'er tried to reach out and grab, but her hand passed through her mother's body, only grasping a warm void in her palm.

The cellar planks closed overhead. A slit was sliced through the light, then it vanished.

In the darkness, only Su Mo'er's rapid breathing and the increasingly clear sounds seeping in from above—hoofing hooves crushing the dry mud, the clanging of metal, and men's shouts. The sounds swarmed in like locusts, buzzing and enveloping the room.

"Look, what kind of junk is that drying in the yard?"

"Pack up all the valuables, don't dawdle!"

Su Mo'er's heart pounded in her chest at the sound, tightening until she could barely breathe.

The wooden steps overhead were shaking as someone stepped onto them and entered the house.

"Oh, and there's a pretty woman too!" The thugs' laughter rang out. Su Mo'er had never heard that kind of laughter before; it was like the growling sound a wolf makes when sizing up its prey, but even more chilling.

"Are you alone?" A voice approached, close enough for her to hear the creaking of boots on the wooden planks. "Tell me first, where do you hide all your valuables?"

The mother didn't answer. The thug spat in boredom, his boots slamming into the ground, followed by a dull thud, like something hitting the floor. Su Mo'er's body jolted. She heard the sound of fabric being torn, her mother's short gasp, and the men's rising and falling laughter and panting. The clatter of a backpack being rummaged through, the crisp sound of a ceramic pot shattering, then more laughter. Someone was kicking something, again and again, a dull, continuous kick, like a fist pounding on a wet sack.

Su Mo'er put her left hand in her mouth, biting down on the web between her thumb and forefinger. Her teeth dug into her flesh, tasting a metallic flavor. Tears streamed down the girl's face, blurring her vision. She trembled, trying her best not to make a sound.

"Let's go, there's nothing here." Someone walked around the house a couple more times, his boots crunching over the broken pottery shards, but he found nothing.

The footsteps faded into the distance. The sound of horses' hooves faded into the distance. The autumn afternoon fell silent again, so quiet that you could hear the whistling of the wind seeping in through the cracks in the door.

Su Mo'er counted her heartbeats in the darkness. Three hundred and twenty-one. She took her hand out of her mouth; a deep ring of teeth marks, oozing blood, marked the base of her thumb. She gently pushed aside the wooden board above her head with that hand.

The light rushed in, so bright that she couldn't open her eyes.

She found herself unable to cry anymore. Her mother lay on the floor, her face turned towards the door, her eyes open, her gaze fixed blankly on the sky. The dark blue bedding, stained with mud and something else, had been pulled to the threshold.

Su Mo'er walked over and knelt down beside her mother. Her hands trembled as she reached out to close her mother's eyes, managing to do so after three attempts. Her mother's face was still warm.

The dried chrysanthemums in the yard were scattered all over the ground, trampled into brown mud by boots. Red dates were rolled everywhere, with a few rolling over the threshold, glistening withered in the sunlight.

Su Mo'er knelt amidst the trampled chrysanthemums, picked up the dark blue quilt, and covered her mother with it. Her hands were covered in blood from her own bites, smeared with dried tears, and her skin was taut and painful.

That night she slept alone in the cellar.

She knew that from that day on, she would never have a childhood again.

But she had to live, even if only for those unspeakable reasons.

The crows in the distant oak tree cawed twice more.

"Mother!"

Su Mo'er woke up with a start, her eyes snapping open.

Before me was the church's grey dome, sunlight streaming in through the windowpanes, filling most of the room. Fine dust particles floated in the air, like ashes in a dream.

She lay in the bedding, her chest heaving violently, a thin layer of sweat beading on her forehead. Her fingertips were still gripping the corner of the blanket tightly, her knuckles turning white.

After a long while, she slowly let go.

It was very quiet outside the church, so quiet that you could clearly hear the whistling of the wind through the torn window paper. Occasionally, a few early birds chirped intermittently under the eaves, their calls short and timid.

Su Mo'er buried her face in the quilt, smelling only the dust and damp cotton, mixed with a little bit of the bitter smell of a burnt candle.

She closed her eyes, her eyes burning hot, but no tears fell.

She couldn't even remember how many days had passed. But every time she closed her eyes, she would return to that afternoon, to that sunniest courtyard, and relive, over and over again, everything that had ripped her heart apart.

Su Mo'er threw back the covers and sat up. The morning light was slowly creeping up her toes, so gentle it made her heart flutter. She stared blankly for a moment, then raised her hand to brush away the tears that had soaked her temples, and struggled to sit up.

The church door was ajar, and a breeze squeezed in through the crack, making a faint sound, like someone humming a tune.

Su Mo'er took a deep breath and stepped out of the door. The air was filled with the smell of soil and wild grass, as well as a certain, uncertain burnt smell that drifted from afar.

Walking down the path outside the church, her feet crunched softly on the gravel. Su Mo'er remembered many years ago, when she had walked out of her own courtyard gate in the same way.

Behind her were crushed chrysanthemums, red dates that had rolled over the threshold, and her mother who had long since passed away.

Everything in the yard seemed to be speaking—the mother's comb, the mother's sewing basket, the old apron that the mother hung on the wall.

But she brought nothing with her at the time; she simply smeared her mother's blood on her forehead.

She never returned to that courtyard after that.

But she never left that courtyard.
