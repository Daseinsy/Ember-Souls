#  Conversations with Sages

He opened his eyes, and the void seemed endless.

The grayish-white mist surged in from all directions and receded in all directions, like the slow breathing of some enormous creature. There was no sky, no earth, no direction, not even the weight of "himself." He looked down to look at his hands, but found the action itself becoming suspicious—he "felt" he had lowered his head, but no hands or body appeared before him, only a continuous, gentle "knowing."

The soul. He thought. So this is what the soul is like. When you're alive, you always imagine your soul as a transparent, ethereal self, recognizable like a reflection in water. But once you truly become a soul, even recognizing yourself loses its coordinates.

"You're awake."

The sound came from the mist, and also from within him, like a pebble falling simultaneously into different parts of water. He looked in the direction of the sound—if that could be called "looking"—and slowly a warmer white mass emerged from the mist, gradually coalescing into a human shape. An old man, draped in a gray robe, his face like a pebble polished by time, each wrinkle rounded and definite. A sage.

"Where is..." he began, only to find that his voice no longer went through his throat and tongue, but vibrated directly in the fog, clearer and more unfamiliar than when he was alive.

“Our dream.” The sage smiled, the wrinkles at the corners of his mouth spreading across his cheeks like tree rings. “A shared dream that has lasted for who knows how long.”

He was silent for a moment, trying to feel his own breath, but only felt a faint drift, as if being carried by water or pushed by the wind, never "arriving".

Am I dead?

"You have broken free from the shackles of the physical body." The sage slowly raised his hand, which had no defined edges, like a wisp of thick fog. "Have you felt liberated?"

Liberation. He repeated the word to himself, like chewing a piece of candy that had long lost its sweetness. The mist passed through his body, bringing an empty coolness. He thought for a moment and said, "I only feel like I'm floating in the void, as if I have nowhere to stand. But I also feel as if I've lost all the burdens on my body. It's just... the ethereal feeling of freedom, everything seems like a dream."

He finished speaking and laughed first. The smile dissipated in the mist, like ice melting on water. Too honest, he thought. In his life, he had never said so much to anyone. But here, in this void without walls or doors, the price of honesty seemed so low. Low enough to bear.

“This is indeed a dream.” The sage nodded, his voice like bubbles rising from a deep well, one after another, slow and full. “It is our dream. A dream woven by all of us.”

He fell silent. The fog flowed between them, sometimes thick, sometimes thin, like countless unformed thoughts.

“I don’t feel happy,” he finally said. “In fact, I’ve never felt safe. I’ve always been running, trying to run faster than others, faster still. I thought that if I ran ahead, I wouldn’t be trampled or left behind. But…” He paused, “I’m not happy.”

The words fell into the fog, without echo. The fog was so thick that even an echo lost its power to return.

The sage gazed at him, his eyes like the gentle afternoon light of autumn, offering no further questions. After a long while, the sage spoke softly, his voice like a falling leaf gliding across the ground: "Take good care of your body, for it is your only thing."

He frowned—if the soul could still frown. A subtle, static-like tremor swept through some part of his consciousness. He didn't understand why the sage was saying this at this moment. The body? He no longer had a body. He had shed that burden. Yet the sage's words were like a tiny thorn, piercing a soft spot deep within his soul, a place where the pain still lingered.

“The gods high above,” he said in a low voice, colder than he had expected, “how can they possibly empathize with the suffering of those at the bottom of society if they have never experienced it?”

The sage did not answer immediately. The fog thickened, like the deepest waters of the night. Then, the sage approached him, his footsteps falling into the void, each step like ripples on the surface of an unseen lake. Those ripples spread outwards, penetrating his soul and bringing a strange, almost body-warm sensation.

“Look.” The sage raised his hand and pointed into the depths of the fog.

He looked in that direction. The fog began to swirl and churn, as if stirred by an unseen hand. Something emerged from the fog, growing brighter and closer—it was a door. A simple, familiar door he had seen countless times before. A rusty lock hung on the door, and the cement along the edges of the frame had peeled away, revealing dark red bricks underneath.

"This is……"

“Yours,” the sage said, “you used to open it every day, and then close it every day.”

He froze. The door in the fog was so clear, so real, as if he could reach out and touch the cold metal handle. He remembered that door. He remembered the cramped space behind it, the direction of every crack in the floor, the perpetual darkness when the curtains were drawn, blurring the lines between day and night. He remembered standing in the doorway, pressing his ear against it, listening for footsteps outside, only daring to turn the key after confirming no one was there.

"You've been running." The sage's voice came from behind, like the tolling of a bell at dusk, steady and distant. "But have you ever thought that what makes you run is your shadow? No matter how fast you run, it will always follow you."

He didn't speak. The image of the door began to sway slightly, like a reflection in water. Mist seeped out from the cracks in the door, wisps of it, like someone had lit a lamp on the other side.

“It’s a kind of spiritual sustenance.” He finally spoke again, his voice so low it was almost swallowed by the mist. He was still staring at the door, something in his eyes slowly tightening, as if he were holding the last key, “because behind me stands an entire world.”

The door trembled in the fog, like an old photograph being blown by the wind. The sage sighed softly, a sigh without pity or judgment, but an echo that was so deep and unfathomable, almost like that of another world.

“So you never truly fell,” the sage said.

He slowly turned his head and looked at the sage. The fog began to thin, dissipating like the receding tide. The void became empty and silent again, but in that emptiness, he "saw" himself for the first time—not his body , but a faint, almost transparent ball of light, hanging there alone after the fog dissipated, like the only light shining in the deep sea.

“Where should I go?” he asked.

The sage smiled without saying a word, but simply raised his hand and pointed again to the door behind him. The lock on the door was gone.

Suddenly he understood. That understanding came without process, without reasoning, like a lightning bolt striking his soul, illuminating every shadow. He turned and floated towards the door. With each step closer, the mist warmed, as if someone had lit a fire on the other side of the door. He reached out, and just as his fingertips were about to touch the door, he heard the sage speak softly behind him, as soft as a seed falling into the earth:

"wake up."

He suddenly opened his eyes.

A very thin crack ran across the ceiling, from the corner to the light fixture. Breathing returned, rapid and heavy. My chest heaved violently, as if I had just surfaced from deep water. Beneath my hands lay the rough, cold sheet, soaked with sweat. The air still carried the lingering smell of coal ash and cheap tobacco, seeping in from a crack in some window. It was still dark. A dog barked once in the distance, then silence returned.

He slowly sat up, burying his face in his hands. That door, that vanished lock. The weightlessness of his soul drifting still lingered in his bones, like salt stains left after the tide recedes. He was alive. He was still alive.

A series of very light footsteps came from outside the door, paused for a few seconds in the pre-dawn darkness, and then walked away.
