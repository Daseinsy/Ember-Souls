#  Caesar

This is the story of a man who is exquisitely self-serving from beginning to end. He climbs his way up from the bottom, possessing exceptional talent and seemingly cynical, yet never snobbish. He can even recognize truly honest friends and is willing to associate with them. However, he is ultimately selfish—because everything he does, in the final analysis, is for his own survival. He can certainly understand those sufferings, even empathize with them, but he never lets his compassion overflow. Once he determines that someone is of no value to him, whether materially or spiritually, he will discard them without hesitation.

Or you could say he's a darker version of Gatsby. He's talented, self-made, and ruthless, yet deep down he retains a sliver of warmth, willing to pursue his own idealized version of love. In fact, he ultimately succeeds. However, once that idealized love becomes an obstacle, he abandons it without hesitation. Perhaps he considers himself superior, and his first love is no longer worthy of his current status; perhaps his affections are fickle, easily lost once obtained; or perhaps he never commits himself to anyone, never offering the kind of exclusive, extreme love. He doesn't believe in moonlight or anything beautiful. Because he deeply understands the ugliness of society, he builds layers of defense to protect himself, including indifference, alcoholism, and even self-exile.

Admittedly, he harbored deep-seated pain due to his frequent drunkenness. But ultimately, he considered himself free, free enough to treat everyone else in the world as insignificant NPCs. However, if you think Caesar was a thoroughly evil person, you'd be mistaken. He was never hypocritical, never pretentious, and never lied to his trusted companions. In fact, the city-states under his rule were always better than before. Perhaps only someone like him could thrive in such a world.

Dialogue is an indispensable detail in literature. Any relationship between people requires some form of dialogue. Of course, dialogue has its limits; sages may need no words, but most humans primarily communicate through the incomplete vessel of language; and on a deeper level, through action. Dialogue, by firmly anchoring itself to the present moment, truly reveals genuine emotions in the details, rather than indulging in grand narratives. Appropriate dialogue and everyday life bring characters to life. But everything has its limits.

Caesar picked up his cigarette, closed his eyes, and gently blew out a puff of smoke before speaking: "I don't know what you're talking about." His expression was strangely calm, as if everything before him were nothing more than statues.

The protagonist stared directly into his eyes. But those eyes were like a bottomless abyss, burying everything beneath them, remaining utterly unmoved. The protagonist didn't respond immediately. A silence fell over the air; neither of them spoke, yet it seemed as if everything had already been said.

The protagonist clenched his fist almost imperceptibly behind his back, took a deep breath, and said, "I understand." His voice was as still as a deep well, like a dead machine. With that, he turned to leave the room.

However, Caesar called out to him.

“We’re friends.” He paused. “Looking forward to the next collaboration.”
