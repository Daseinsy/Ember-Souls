#  Introducing the Sage

%%
Objective: To introduce the group of sages, embodying communication and the light of humanity in the end times.

The wilderness paths forged by the sages became bridges connecting the city-states during the Age of Night. So what were these sages like in the beginning? They might have been unshaven uncles, long-bearded old men, kind-faced grandmothers, precocious young people, and countless nameless individuals. They used their bodies as shields, their spirits standing tall for millennia, and more and more people joined the ranks of the sages.

Sages are known as those who ignite the flame of civilization. Their spirituality is extraordinary; though still mortal, they accomplish what most gods have failed to do or are unwilling to do. Their spirit is so moving that heaven and earth remember them. Though their physical bodies have long since perished, their spirits seem immortal; as long as civilization exists, their echoes will resonate through time and space. Most of them are nameless, but their spirit lives on forever. Humans, through cultivation or enlightenment, may randomly resonate with them, experiencing a qualitative leap in their spirituality, their consciousness entering a realm of eternity. Here, time flows no further; even a long period of cultivation in this state feels like only a fleeting moment in the outside world. This is entering a dream, or spiritual communion, annihilating time and the past. This is called discussing the Dao.

No one knows why the wise have survived; perhaps they have always existed in the collective consciousness of humanity, a result of being retained subconsciously.

Surprisingly, none of these sages left their names; even if you told them their names in life, they never claimed them. Some sages became saints simply by observing a river.

When the wind comes from the north, it carries the smell of rust and ash.

Roland pulled his scarf up higher, covering his mouth and nose. The scarf was left to him by his mother; it was wool mixed with some soft but durable fiber, dark gray, and the edges were already frayed. It was the seventh day he had been walking this road, and the blisters on the soles of his feet had grown into his flesh, making each step feel like stepping on someone else's wounds.

The road was wide. The surface was made of a material he couldn't quite identify—neither stone nor metal, but a greyish-white with a warm, matte finish, like the skeleton of some enormous creature. At intervals along both sides of the road stood stone pillars, their surfaces densely covered with runes, emitting an extremely faint, almost extinguished blue light. In the boundless darkness, this blue light was dimmer than that of fireflies.

But the road still stands. This road, which traverses two city-states, crosses the wasteland once known as "Death Valley," and remains steadfast thirty-three years after the sun went out, is called "The Wilderness Road."

Roland stopped and took out his water pouch from his backpack. The pouch was half deflated. He carefully took a small sip, letting the water linger in his mouth for a moment, moistening his tongue and chapped lips, before slowly swallowing. He looked up at the sky. The sky was a vast dome, with an artificial sun hanging in the far distance, like a flickering orange bulb fixed to a black cloth, tinging the clouds at its edges with a dark red hue. The light barely reached the ground, illuminating everything in a half-light, half-dark state, as if forever trapped in twilight.

They call this the "ember moment." In the days after the sun goes out, an artificial sun replaces the old star, but it is too far away and too dim, like the last heartbeat of a dying person.

"How much further?" Roland asked.

There was no one with him.

This is a forbidden road. Although the Wilderness Road is called a bridge, very few people actually dare to tread this path. There is a barrier left by sages on the Wilderness Road; the stone pillars are the anchor points, forming an invisible barrier on both sides of the road, keeping the chaos and darkness outside the city-state at bay. But within the barrier, the world remains desolate and lifeless, like a corridor crossing the River Styx.

Roland continued walking. His footsteps were exceptionally clear in the emptiness, each step like a tap on the ground, which always responded with silence. He walked for a long time. Or perhaps not so long. On this road, the flow of time was strange. His shadow, stretched thin and long in the embers, was like a frail, silent companion, always half a step ahead of him.

Then, he saw the old man.

The old man sat on a rock by the roadside, leaning against the guardrail at the edge of the road. The guardrail was actually a fallen stone pillar, half-buried in the ground, its runes completely extinguished. The old man wore a long, coarse gray robe, and his beard was long, reaching his waist like a clump of withered grass. His face was covered in deep wrinkles, as deep as land parched for centuries. But when Roland saw his eyes, he paused, startled.

Those eyes were bright. They didn't match the weathered look on their faces at all; they were like two still-warm stars.

"You've come," the old man said, his voice hoarse but carrying a strange composure, as if he had been waiting there for a long time.

"Are you... a sage?" Roland stopped in his tracks, not daring to approach rashly.

The old man didn't answer, but simply raised his hand and gestured: "Sit down. The journey is long, a little longer won't make a difference."

Roland hesitated. He really didn't have time. But there was something about the old man, something heavier than his own anxiety, that made it impossible for him to refuse. He walked over and sat down three steps away from the old man.

"Where are you going by taking this road?" the old man asked.

“Go to the city-state to the east,” Roland said. “My sister is there; she…is ill.”

What kind of illness?

“The fever won’t go down. The witch doctors say it’s spiritual exhaustion. They can’t cure it. I’ve heard that there’s an Awakened One in the eastern city-state who can guide people’s spiritual paths through spiritual resonance.” Roland’s voice lowered. “I don’t know if it’s true. But they say the Wilderness Path can take you anywhere, as long as you’re on the right path.”

The old man nodded, gazing ahead, his eyes piercing through the road and the darkness, as if he could see a place very, very far away.

"Do you know who built this road?" the old man asked.

“The Sages,” Roland said. “Everyone knows that. The Sages forged the paths through the wilderness with their lives, allowing the city-states of the Dark Ages to remain connected. They are known as ‘those who ignite the flame of civilization.’”

"Oh?" The old man's voice rose slightly, tinged with amusement. "Have you ever met a sage?"

Roland shook his head.

“I’ve seen him before.” The old man withdrew his gaze and landed on Roland. He was silent for a moment, as if trying to retrieve those images from the depths of his memory. Then he spoke slowly, his voice echoing in the empty space, like the wind blowing through ancient stone walls.

"The first one was a middle-aged man. He was unkempt, with a scraggly beard, and dressed like a beggar. He wasn't very talkative and had a strange temper. Every time someone asked him why he was building the road, he would yell at them. Some people thought he was crazy. But one night, the Dark Realm surged up, and black mist spread from the other side of the canyon, freezing the air wherever it went. He stood at the front of the group, arms outstretched, like a fool. Then his body disappeared. Only light remained. A blue light that froze the entire road in place. His shadow was in that light."

Roland listened quietly.

“The second one was an old woman.” The old woman slowed her speech, as if savoring each word. “She was older than me. She was very ordinary, so ordinary that you could see her ten times at the market and still not remember her face. Her method of paving the road was the most rudimentary. She moved the stones one by one, using her own spirit to connect them, and could only pave a small section the size of a palm a day. Some people said she was too slow, but she didn’t say anything, she just kept paving. Later she died. When she died, she was still in the posture of paving the road, her hand pressed on the road surface, as if she wanted to press her last bit of spirit into it. Her body decayed quickly, but the section of road surface under her fingers can still be felt warm to this day.”

Roland unconsciously glanced down at the ground beneath his feet. The grayish-white ground was cold. But he suddenly had an urge to put his hand on it and test it.

“More and more people went,” the old man said. “Precocious young people, silent travelers, craftsmen burdened with the stigma of being fugitives but actually innocent… They went one after another. No one commanded them, no one paid them. They only knew that if this road wasn’t built, the city-states would become isolated islands, and the last flame of civilization would be extinguished. So they went. Most didn’t even leave their names. On the day the Wilderness Road was completed, there was no celebration, no record, not even a proper inscription. Only the wind.” The old man’s voice suddenly softened, like a sigh. “The wind remembered for them.”

Roland remained silent. He opened his mouth, as if to say something, but his throat felt constricted. He remembered his mother telling him stories by the oil lamp when he was a child, saying that the wise men were "so moving that even heaven and earth remembered them." He hadn't understood then, thinking it was just an ancient legend. But now, sitting on the edge of the desolate road, listening to the old man's hoarse voice, he suddenly felt as if he heard something. The voice was far away, coming from the other end of the road, seeping from the runes on every stone pillar, like countless people at different times and in different places, whispering the same vow.

“I think… I can feel it,” Roland said softly.

The old man glanced at him, a slight smile playing on his lips: "That's because you put your heart into it. This road will remember everyone who walks it with care."

“But why don’t the wise men leave their names?” Roland asked. “They did such great things, yet they don’t even have names. People in later generations don’t even know who they are.”

The old man was silent for a moment. He closed his eyes, as if feeling the touch of the wind blowing from the north. Then he slowly said, “Names are for the living. They don’t need them anymore. They exist in the collective consciousness of humanity, they’ve always been there. Can you call them by name? You can’t. They don’t care. They only care about one thing—whether you hear their echo as you walk this road.”

Roland looked up. Just then, he saw the light.

On both sides of the road, the runes on the stone pillars that had been extinguished suddenly lit up. Not very bright, still that fading blue, but this time they seemed to come alive, lighting up little by little, like someone opening their eyes in the dark. Then, Roland felt a warm surge rising from the soles of his feet, flowing into his calves , thighs, waist, abdomen, and chest, finally exploding into a silent flower of light between his brows. His consciousness was lifted up, as if enveloped by a warm stream. All the fatigue, thirst, the blisters on his feet, the soreness in his knees, were instantly washed away.

His consciousness soared high. He saw the road. The entire road, winding in the darkness, was like a river emitting blue light, flowing through canyons, through wastelands, through abandoned cities, connecting one city-state after another. There were people on the road. Many people. There were travelers hurrying along, caravans on tours, messengers on ponies, and pilgrims with dust-covered faces. They were all walking. And on both sides of the road, the blue light shone like stars, endless, stretching from one end to the other.

Roland felt he was no longer alone. He was connected to the entire wilderness road. He could feel the heartbeat of every traveler, hear the resonance of every stone pillar, and touch the warmth of each stone on the road. And in the deepest part, he touched something older, something more profound, like a sleeping dragon, lying in the depths of time, its breath carrying the joys and sorrows of countless generations. It was collective consciousness, the deepest roots of human civilization in this world of death.

He realized that the sages were there. They had never left. They had simply paved the way, transformed into light, and become an inextinguishable anchor in the depths of human consciousness. They witnessed the first human emerge from the cave, and they would surely witness the last human close his eyes.

His consciousness drifted in this river for a long time. Or perhaps only for a moment. Time lost its meaning here.

When he woke up, the wind was still blowing. He was still sitting by the roadside, his nose red from the cold, his breath condensing into white mist in front of him. But the old man was gone. On the stone where the old man had been sitting, only a withered yellow leaf remained. The leaf swirled gently in the wind, like a hand waving.

Roland stood up. He felt incredibly light, as if he had donned a new set of clothes. The blisters on his feet were still there, but he no longer felt the pain. He glanced back at the path he had come from, then at the endless road ahead. This time, he was no longer lost. He knew his sister would recover. He knew this road would lead him to where he was meant to be. Because for a fleeting moment, he had shared eternity with this road.

He strode forward. The footsteps sounded again, this time the ground seemed to respond. One after another, as if someone was walking alongside him at the end of the road.
