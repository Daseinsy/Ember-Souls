#  Evil God

Seventy-two Evil Gods

The world possesses seventy-two evil gods, considered the absolute supreme beings among them, each an ultimate source of terror. They did not appear simultaneously, but rather emerged one by one over a long period of time. Each corresponds to a fundamental human desire—gluttony, greed, sloth, envy, pride, lust, and wrath are the seven most famous, but there are many more. There is also an evil god named "Oblivion," who feeds on human memories; an evil god named "Echo," who feeds on unspoken words; and an evil god named "Mirror," who feeds on human self-doubt. The list of the seventy-two evil gods is incomplete, as some have never appeared; their existence is only perceived.

Every evil god is rooted in a desire. Driven by this desire, they enslave humanity, helping them eliminate evil spirits in exchange for the people's offerings and faith. They are not entirely cruel to the creatures under their rule; as long as their desires are satisfied, they don't mind doing good deeds. Therefore, in some regions, people would rather worship evil gods than be collectively exiled to the unprotected wilderness—the "hope" offered by the evil gods, though faint, is real. But the truth is, all of this is merely a means for those in power to further exploit and exploit.

The Sorrow of the Wilderness

The most dangerous thing in the wilderness isn't the darkness or evil spirits, but the "sorrow" that erodes the spirit. It's a slow, invisible spiritual decay—like salt seeping into water. Imperceptibly, your will becomes dull, your emotions thin, and your memories begin to blur. At first, it's just a slight discomfort, like a sleepy morning ; then comes persistent fatigue, like traveling for three days straight; finally, utter numbness. You no longer know who you are, no longer remember why you came here. By then, you are no longer yourself—you have become part of the wilderness, either a wandering evil spirit or a crawling monster.

There is a time limit for staying in the wilderness; after a certain period, one must return to the place where the sacred fire burns to reset their spiritual state. Therefore, explorers who enter the wilderness carry a simple timer—not a clock, but a small piece of spiritual stone soaked in sacred fire. The color of the spiritual stone slowly darkens as its spirituality is eroded; when it darkens to a certain threshold, it means one must return, or there will be no return. Experienced wilderness hunters allow themselves double the return time, because no one knows what they will encounter in the wilderness, whether they will get lost, or what will delay them.

The truth of the labyrinth

The Labyrinth is a gateway to various worlds, hidden deep in the wilderness or shrouded in mist. The Empire marks it as a red zone, deploying troops to seal it off, but this only fuels more curiosity. Every few years, fearless individuals attempt to traverse the Labyrinth—some return, driven mad; others never return. Legend has it that the Labyrinth's end is home to an ultimate demon, one to which even those at the highest level of power have failed to return. But there is a more elusive theory: the Labyrinth is actually a rift left by Ragnarok, and traversing it doesn't take you to another place, but to another time—perhaps the past, perhaps the future, perhaps a possibility that never happened.

Evil spirits are for destruction, while evil gods are for enslavement.

The essence of an evil god is a human or other being who has stolen the authority of the gods. After Ragnarok, countless fragments of divine authority were scattered throughout the world. Some of these fragments were collected and refined by those with ulterior motives, thus giving birth to evil gods. Evil gods do not possess the complete authority and divinity of true gods; they are merely thieves who hitch a ride.

There are seventy-two renowned evil gods in the world, considered the absolute supreme among them, each representing ultimate terror and a specific desire. Some evil gods established city-states, disguising themselves as benevolent deities, setting up churches, priests, and prayer rituals to earn the worship of their inhabitants; some dwell in forbidden lands, occasionally releasing pollution; others roam the wilderness, the deep sea, and the void, seeking new prey. Each evil god is rooted in a single desire—greed, gluttony, lust, power, fear, deception… These desires are the source of their power. Driven by these desires, the evil gods enslave humanity, helping them eliminate evil spirits in exchange for their offerings and faith. They are not entirely cruel to the creatures under their rule; as long as their desires are satisfied, the evil gods are willing to do good deeds. Therefore, in some regions, people would rather worship evil gods than be collectively exiled to the unprotected wilderness—the "hope" offered by the evil gods, though faint, is undeniably real. The truth is, all of this is merely a means for those in power to further exploit and exploit.

What are benevolent gods? Perhaps they are gods who uphold good deeds. But benevolent gods can also do evil, or rather, they may eliminate reason in the name of justice. The city-states under the rule of benevolent gods are vast, violence is forbidden, and people can only survive through legal means and intense competition. A few regions are indeed truly controlled by benevolent gods, but these are extremely rare. In most areas, the "righteous gods" are merely well-disguised evil gods. They are not as vicious as other evil gods simply because "sustainable harvesting" is more in line with long-term interests than "one-time exploitation." People are unaware of this—they believe it to be good.

Besides evil gods, there exists an even more clandestine entity: God Thieves. They impersonate deceased deities to collect the power of faith, and once a believer is devout enough, they can steal fragments of the corresponding deity's remaining authority in the world. This is considered a grave crime across all seven continents—because the path to godhood should be a ladder paved by living beings through cultivation and merit, while God Thieves seek to hitch a ride. They claim they are merely reclaiming abandoned legacies: since the gods have fallen, why can't their authority change hands? God Thieves typically possess exceptional intelligence and the ability to disguise themselves; they hide in churches, academies, noble mansions, and may even preach in the square of a bustling city. Identifying God Thieves is extremely difficult because the gods they imitate truly existed, and their doctrines are not entirely false.

There exists a subtle rivalry between the evil god and the god thieves. The evil god forbids anyone from encroaching on its followers and territory, while the god thieves specialize in undermining the evil god. The covert battle between the two never ceases, but the common people remain forever unaware.

Some lords sided with evil spirits or gods, falsely labeling the awakened as "heretics" and burning them at the stake. These young people simply saw things they shouldn't have seen one day—for example, a figure passing by outside their window that turned out to be a neighbor who had died three days prior. The lords' lackeys craved immortality, believing that the gifts of evil spirits or gods could allow them to transcend the ironclad law of lifespan, even at the cost of becoming a mere shell in human skin. Continents ruled by such lords were typically isolated and at war with other continents—because war perpetually generated suffering and death, and suffering was the food of evil spirits, while death was the breeding ground for new evil spirits. Isolation combined with war made such regions the perfect breeding ground for evil gods.
