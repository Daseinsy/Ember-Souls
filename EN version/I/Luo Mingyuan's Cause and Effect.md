#  Luo Mingyuan's Cause and Effect

Luo Ming was cautious in everything he did, always striving to be the best. He couldn't stand falling behind others and always wanted to be number one. He hated his past, vulnerable self and even avoided mentioning it. He had been driven by this force since childhood. Why did he develop this abnormal mentality? Perhaps it was because his weak and withdrawn personality or his unhappy life led him to suddenly derive pleasure from comparison, causing him to lose his mind and cling to it as a lifeline. He was afraid of falling, and to avoid falling, he was even willing to sacrifice his conscience.
It's ridiculous, isn't it? But that's how he got here before, and he can only stay number one in the future because being number one is an anchor point for his spirit; otherwise, he will fall apart.
Yes, his pursuit of first place reached a pathological, ruthless level, but it was driven by reality. He came from a slum; if he didn't come in first, he would die in some deserted corner; if he didn't come in first, his family and friends would look down on him. If he didn't come in first, his mother would die, because he had been playing and hadn't come in first, so he hadn't received the prize money and hadn't brought his mother to the large house with accompanying family members as planned, thus preventing her from being killed by evil spirits. From then on, Luo Mingyuan's obsession with coming in first became a malaise. He had no parents, no relatives; only by coming in first could he gain immense pleasure. He also enjoyed the awe in others' eyes and the admiration in his wife and children's eyes.
He could sense that shadow in Lin Jing, so he paid special attention to her. They were both people who were afraid of falling down deep down. But Lin Jing was more sensitive, more meticulous, and had more principles and bottom lines, so he always thought more and lived a tiring life.
While Luo Mingyuan forced himself to do things he didn't like, Lin Jing simply gave up. He was more Zen-like and indifferent because he always knew that his soul was already burning out and couldn't withstand any more overdraft.

The lecture hall door was ajar, and the afternoon breeze swirled in dried flowers from the locust trees under the eaves, brushing against the ankles of the last row of students before rolling out again. Luo Mingyuan stood behind the podium, his fingertips resting on an open scroll of the "Spiritual Vein Division Chart." He spoke at a steady pace, pausing after each sentence to glance at the students below. Only when all the heads turned to look at him did he continue to the next page of the chart.

"The third branch of the meridian sequence, go sense it, it's right there. The biggest advantage of beginners in the lower city is their stronger physical adaptability. You've been used to doing rough work since you were little, so your spiritual meridians are more resilient to pressure than the pampered children in the upper city—" He paused, his gaze sweeping over the lingering chilblains on the back of a girl's hand in the front row, the swollen, purplish-red skin... "That's talent."

Forty-odd pairs of eyes lit up below. The things held in those eyes were all too familiar; he could picture them with his eyes closed: the almost painful longing of a young man raised in a coal-ash alley when he first saw the shimmering patterns of light on a spiritual cultivator's robe. Now, those gazes absorbed every word he spoke, like parched earth craving rain. Some gripped their charcoal pencils, frantically memorizing; some tilted their heads back, holding their breath; some smiled involuntarily, an uncontrollable upturn, as if they could already see themselves standing on some high platform, wearing a spiritual cultivator's robe trimmed with silver.

Luo Mingyuan turned to page twenty-seven. The diagram depicted a spiritual vein that had shed its mortal nature and was completely clear, with the words "Great Transformation Realm, Initial Stage" written in gilded characters below it. His fingertip paused on those words for a moment.

"Those of you present, if you can complete the first stage of the cultivation process within three years, you may apply to transfer to the Inner Academy Preparatory Department." He continued calmly, "At that time, you will receive a monthly allocation of spirit stones, a supply of spirit food, and access to the lowest level of the library—"

A thin boy in the back row suddenly raised his hand. Luo Mingyuan recognized him; he had handed in a paper full of mistakes during the theory test last week, but the mistakes were very neat, and every stroke showed an excessive devotion.

"Sir," the boy's voice was hoarse, typical of puberty, "once it gets to that point, will it be possible to get my family to move out of Coal Ash Alley in the lower city?"

A moment of silence fell over the hall. A few people chuckled, but the laughter was brief. Luo Mingyuan looked at the boy, at the frayed edge of the fabric inside his collar, repeatedly starched, and at an old scar above his collarbone, rubbed raw by a carrying pole—a scar he had once had exactly the same, on his left shoulder blade, from when he was fifteen, carrying two baskets of coal up a hill, the rope snapping, the baskets falling down and scraping off a layer of skin. Later, spiritual cleansing had faded that scar, and he could no longer feel it.

He turned his face and stared for a moment at the sliver of light and shadow on the pillar outside the window.

"It can be moved. And not just moved." He closed the map, slung it over his shoulder, and paced a few steps. "Six miles outside Coal Ash Alley, in the Red Brick Market, there's a single-story house with a yard. The monthly rent is only three low-grade spirit stones. That's more than enough for the scholarship I receive from the academy after completing the first stage of the Qi Refining process."

The boy smiled, revealing two uneven front teeth, his whole face glowing with a smile. A girl with pigtails next to him nudged him with her elbow, and the two exchanged a glance—a look Luo Mingyuan had seen countless times before—like two match heads touching, ready to ignite at the slightest touch.

"but."

He suddenly spoke. That one word seemed to lower the temperature in the hall slightly. He turned back to the podium, his palm resting on the scroll of illustrations, his knuckles turning slightly white.

"What are your plans after you move out?"

The boy paused, then said, "Continue your cultivation, advance to the second meridian sequence, and then—"

"Then what."

"Then graduate, become a full-fledged spiritualist, and receive a stipend—"

"And then what?"

The boy opened his mouth, but no sound came out. The girl next to him continued, "And then you can live a good life, sir. No more eating coal dust, no more carrying loads, no more sores on your hands and feet in winter—"

Luo Mingyuan looked at the back of her hand, which was still covered in chilblains. The girl suddenly realized where he was looking and hurriedly pulled her arm back under the table, her face turning bright red.

"Good days." He repeated the three words, the last syllable rising slightly, neither a question nor a statement. Then he smiled, a very faint smile that only moved the corners of his mouth, his eyes remaining perfectly still. "You all understand the meaning of 'good days' very well, that's good. Now, that's all for today's lesson."

The students gradually stood up. The sound of desk and chair legs scraping the floor receded like a receding tide, leaving faint white scratches on the wooden floor. Luo Mingyuan stood still, his fingers leaving two light red marks on the scroll of illustrations.

As the skinny boy reached the door, he suddenly turned back and, as if summoning his courage, said to him, "Sir, next time I promise the talismans I submit will be perfect."

Luo Mingyuan nodded. The boy revealed his two crooked teeth, turned around, and ran out.

The lecture hall was empty. A draft rustled the few scattered pages of lecture notes on the podium. He pressed a corner of the paper down, looking down at his hand—smooth skin, nimble and strong fingers, the veins beneath shimmering with a faint silvery-blue sheen. He flipped his wrist, palm up, and the light spread across his palm, illuminating the lines with exquisite detail. The traces of the coal ash alley had long since died here, along with the scar on his left shoulder blade, many years ago.

He returned to his private office in the inner courtyard. As the door closed, he leaned against it and closed his eyes for a moment. A bronze mirror stood against the wall, its surface polished to an extremely smooth finish, reflecting the image of a middle-aged man with slightly high cheekbones and clear, bright eyes. He had no gray hair at his temples, his back was straight as a pine tree, and the silver trim on his robes still gleamed faintly in the shadows.

The person in the mirror is very beautiful. The kind of beauty that even the children of the coal ash alley couldn't capture in a whole night's dream.

He stared at his reflection in the mirror for a few moments, then looked away and walked to the desk. A stack of team progress reports lay on the desk, awaiting signature. The top one, marked in vermilion ink, read, "Exceeded the third node target – psionic energy recovery rate increased by four percent." His deputy, Zhao Qin, had added a note at the end of the report: "Master Luo, you slept in the workshop again last night. Take a day off."

Luo Mingyuan pulled out the report, scratched the words "take a day off" with his fingernail, and put it back on the corner of the table. On the other side of the table was a celadon bowl, with the soup from this morning's spiritual meal still at the bottom, which had solidified into a translucent gel. He picked it up and drank it all in one gulp. The cooled spiritual meal slid down his throat with a dull feeling of fullness, like swallowing a piece of ice.

He sat down at his desk, pulled out a new sheet of talisman paper, and began revising the process diagrams for the next stage. The pen moved across the paper, spiritual energy flowing from his fingertips into the ink, leaving behind fine, silvery-blue specks. This was the team he had personally built—seventeen people—who, in four months, had transformed a discarded spiritual energy gathering array into the academy's most efficient recycling system. At the academy's administrative meeting, someone patted him on the shoulder and said, "Master Luo is really working hard." He didn't reply, but simply handed over the budget sheet for the next phase.

Of course he fought desperately. He had no choice but to fight desperately. When he climbed up, there were no ropes behind him; every step was taken by digging his fingernails into the cracks in the rock, chiseling until he was bloody and mangled. He would then drink spiritual food, stand up as if he were brand new, and continue chiseling. How ruthless was he to himself? Last winter, his spiritual veins were overloaded, causing hemiplegia. He lay on the workshop floor for four hours, his teeth chattering from the cold, until dawn when his spiritual energy returned on its own, allowing him to get up again and sign the three progress reports for the day. Later, his assistant noticed that he looked unwell and wanted to call a spiritual physician, but he waved his hand and said, "It's nothing, I'm fine now."

He's healed. Everything is healed. Spiritual energy was poured in, his meridians were unblocked, and his damaged body and severed nerves were regenerated overnight. Every inch of his body had been replaced more than once, to the point that he himself couldn't remember which parts were his original mortal body and which parts were new shells repeatedly forged by spiritual energy.

He finished drawing the last line of the process, put down his pen, and rubbed his temples. Dusk settled in the courtyard outside the window, and lanterns along the corridor lit up one by one, bathing the cobblestone path in a warm, sugary glow. He had walked this path for over a decade, from preparatory course to formal editor, from editor to team leader, from team leader to his current chair professor position. He recognized every single stone slab; he knew which ones were cracked, which ones were puddled after rain. He could walk to the end with his eyes closed.

end.

He suddenly remembered the expression on the boy's face when he asked "And then what?" that afternoon—that blankness wasn't ignorance, it was trust. He trusted that something better was waiting ahead, trusted that the top of that staircase was a bright platform.

Luo Mingyuan stood up and walked to the window. His face was reflected in the windowpane, the same one in the bronze mirror, but the twilight cast a shadow, making the hollows under his cheekbones appear deeper. He stared at that face, feeling that something was looking out from within it—not his eyes, but something deeper, like a bubble rising from the bottom of a well.

He remembered the dream he'd had last night. In the dream, he was back to being fifteen, a coal basket weighing down his shoulder pole. The rope had snapped, and the edge of the basket had pierced his shoulder blade. He knelt on the slope, writhing in pain, unable to utter a sound. The astringent taste of the dust filling his mouth was so vivid it didn't seem like a dream. He struggled to get up, his knees trembling. At the top of the slope stood a person, dressed in the silver-trimmed robes of a spiritualist, looking down at him. He looked up, trying to see the person's face, but the sun was too bright, and in the backlight, only a dark silhouette remained.

The person lifted their foot and walked away.

He woke up at this point in his dream. When he woke, there was a small damp patch on his pillow. He reached out and touched it; it was cold, and he couldn't tell if it was cold sweat or something else. He sat in the darkness for a moment, trying to recall what he had dreamt about, but he couldn't remember any details, only a ghostly ache in his shoulder blade—the lingering phantom limb sensation of an old injury long since healed. He turned over and fell into a deep sleep again, forgetting everything when he woke up the next day.

Standing by the window, that dream suddenly resurfaced. The backlit figure at the top of the slope, the silver trim on his own robes. He tugged at the corner of his mouth, suppressing the image.

The report on the desk shone faintly in the twilight. He walked over and signed his name below the words "exceeded expectations," his pen sharp and forceful. After signing, he put down his pen, leaned back in his chair, and slowly mobilized the spiritual energy in his meridians to nourish his internal organs. He could complete his daily practice with his eyes closed.

He remembered many years ago, back in Coal Ash Alley, there was a family at the alley entrance who owned a lame dog. Every time the dog was kicked, it would whimper and hide under the eaves, but it would come out again the next day, still wagging its tail at every living thing that passed by. He squatted at the alley entrance for a long time, unable to understand how a dog could be kicked so many times and still wag its tail. Later, the dog died, before the first snow of winter fell, and someone dragged it to the mass grave outside the city. He never stopped for any lame dog again after that.

Spiritual practitioners emphasize not interfering in the karma of others. This is never taught in class, but in private discussions within the inner courtyard, senior monks, sipping spiritual tea, will slowly say: "Too many karmic entanglements lead to entanglement in negative karma, hindering any progress in cultivation. If you help someone, you become involved in a segment of their fate; if you save a beast, all its future encounters become intricately intertwined with yours. Wise people don't do such things. Wise people withdraw their hands, close their doors, and guard the purity of their own spiritual channels."

Luo Mingyuan had been listening to these words for over a decade. At first, he felt something was off, but later he got used to them, and they became like background noise that didn't require his attention. He truly stopped shedding tears for those creatures. One night, when he passed by the winter relief tents in the lower city and smelled a mixture of musty grass and frostbite ointment, he simply quickened his pace without even glancing at the tent entrance.

Not interfering with cause and effect. Good reason. He accepted it faster than he expected.

He opened his eyes. Spiritual energy flowed smoothly through his meridians, his internal organs radiating a warm, nourished comfort. He looked down at his hands, clasping them together, opening them again, and clasping them together once more. These hands now influenced the efficiency of the entire academy's spiritual energy system through the reports they signed, the runes they drew, and the array sequences they revised. A single decision he made could allow a team of seventeen to catch up on their work by three more days, and could double the monthly surplus of the spiritual kitchen's rations. The karmic entanglements were already numerous, forming a dense, intricate web that encompassed himself, his family, his reputation, and the position he now depended on for every breath.

He was afraid of taking on even more.

But the thin boy's eyes were still burning on his retina. The burning made the scar on his shoulder blade, which had already disappeared, start to itch again. He didn't scratch it, but instead poured that itch into the lines of the next process drawing, making the pen tip move faster and harder.

Night birds fluttered past the window. He suddenly wondered: when the Holy Spirit Siddhartha established channels of communication between city-states, and when the wise Nietzsche vowed to liberate all beings in hell, were they ever afraid of interfering in the karma of others?

The thought flashed through his mind so quickly that he didn't even have time to recognize it before it was cut off. He rubbed his temples, folded the signed report and put it into the document box, then leaned back in his chair, closed his eyes, and began the second round of spiritual vein nurturing for the evening.

A breeze seeped in through the window cracks, carrying the scent of dry, late autumn grass. His breathing was long and even, his face as serene as ever, like a true, impeccable role model.

Only he knew that where that thought had just ended, a faint hum lingered. Like water rippling at the bottom of a deep well, it was quickly suppressed by the deeper, thicker darkness, without even a ripple reaching the surface.

In the eyes of most students and colleagues, Luo Mingyuan was an understanding and gentle person, but he was not a person with a pure and innocent heart, or had ever possessed such a heart. In the end, he chose to protect himself and his family rather than his ideals.
