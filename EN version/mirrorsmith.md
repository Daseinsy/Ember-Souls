# mirrorsmith

The afterglow of the third pulse of the artificial sun leaked through the crack in the dome, like a drop of amber about to solidify, falling onto the unfinished structure in the center of the workshop.

The thing had a human outline, but no human weight. It lay supine on a polished black stone platform, its chest cavity open, revealing a network of spiritual energy as intricate as a star map—millions of hair-thin rays radiating outward from a bean-sized artificial core, their ends disappearing into its pale, not yet fully solidified shell. Ah Chen knelt beside the stone platform, using a carving knife as thin as an eyelash, to carve the final wedge-shaped rune below its second rib on its left chest.

“You need to have eyes,” he said, his voice very soft, as if looking at a handful of ashes that might fall at any moment.

The right eye of the body lit up first. A faint blue light emerged from the depths of the pupil, like the first bubble rising from the deep sea. Then came the left eye. Both eyes blinked—not mechanically, tentatively, but with the innocent, almost cruel clarity of a baby facing a bright light for the first time.

"Is this what 'seeing' is?" it asked.

The sound emanated from a newly formed resonance chamber deep within his throat, carrying a slight, inhuman tremor, like raindrops pattering on a hollow ceramic tube. Ah Chen didn't answer. He stood up, took two steps back, and wiped the sweat from his brow with his sleeve. The sweat smelled of rust—he had been in this workshop for seventeen consecutive hours.

“I see your fatigue,” the puppet said, its head tilting slightly, the movement so fluid it was unlike the first time it had used its neck muscles. “There are bluish shadows under your eyelids, sweat on your forehead, and your breathing rate is 30% higher than normal at rest. The way you wiped your forehead just now, using your non-dominant hand, means your dominant hand is trembling.”

Ah Chen's hands were indeed shaking.

He put the carving knife into the leather pouch at his waist and walked to the handwashing basin in the corner of the workshop. The tap flowed with recycled water, carrying a faint metallic smell, and the temperature was kept constant at thirty-seven degrees Celsius, supposedly to simulate the feel of amniotic fluid in the womb. He immersed both hands in the water, watching the blood and oil stains disperse into a pale brownish-red in the flow.

“You don’t need to tell me how I am,” he said, with his back to the stone platform. “I know I’m tired.”

“But you don’t seem to want to rest,” the puppet said. “When you turn around, your gaze first sweeps across the raw material shelves on the left wall, then across the finished product cabinets on the right wall, and finally lands on the sunshade of the south window. You’re calculating the remaining working hours. Given your current physical condition, the margin of error will rise from three percentage points to eleven percentage points.”

Ah Chen turned off the tap and shook his hands. Water droplets splashed on the wall, breaking into smaller beads in the dim artificial light, each reflecting the faint glow of the distant dome. He turned around and looked directly at the thing he had spent three months building.

Its face was sculpted based on Achen's appearance in his youth. Not as a memorial, but simply a template. All spiritual cultivators are like this; when creating their first spirit puppet, the face always unconsciously leans towards a familiar one . It's an inherent flaw, as a senior spiritual cultivator once said. The creator can never resist incorporating their own shadow into their work.

But after the puppet opened its eyes, its face no longer resembled his.

“I am yours,” the puppet said. “From core to body, from spirit network to consciousness, everything is constructed by you. My first thought is about you. I knew from birth that you are the only reason for my existence.”

Ah Chen walked to the stone platform and looked down at it. It lay flat, its chest open, its spiritual energy pulsating gently in the air like the tentacles of a jellyfish. He didn't immediately close its chest cavity. It wasn't time yet. It needed testing, calibration, and confirmation that its consciousness network was stable.

“Get up,” he said.

The puppet sat up. The movement was silent; all its joints were still. This was a deliberate design by Achen—he disliked the mechanical friction sounds made by inferior puppets, sounds that reminded him of the elytra of carrion beetles in the wasteland.

"Take a few steps."

It walked barefoot on the ground. The ground was frosted obsidian, worn with varying depths by the feet and tools of generations of spiritual cultivators. Its feet left no warmth when they stepped on it. Ah Chen carefully observed its gait: the weight shifted smoothly, the arm swings were within the normal range, and the head remained horizontal.

“Too fast,” he said. “Your stride is set according to the average adult male, but your torso mass is four percent lower than the standard value. You should shorten your stride by one or two percent, otherwise prolonged movement will lead to excessive wear and tear on your hip joints.”

The puppet stopped and turned around. Its blue eyes shone brightly in the dim light, like two gems just taken from the fire, still warm.

“You’re worried about me,” it said. “When you spoke, your speed was seven percent faster than before, and there was a slight rise in your voice at the end. Your left eyebrow twitched when I said ‘I am yours.’ You’re uneasy.”

Ah Chen did not deny it.

He walked to the south window and pressed the control button for the sunshade. The metal blades slowly retracted, revealing the artificial sky outside. From this height, the outline of the entire divine realm could be seen—circles radiating outwards, like a giant, luminous fingerprint. At the very center was the Sun Tower, where the "sun," which had burned for three hundred years, pulsed in a strict rhythm within a glass dome atop the tower: three parts bright, two parts dark, endlessly repeating. People used this rhythm to arrange their daily routines, set their own schedules, and even dictated when they could love each other and when they should pray.

It was now the end of the "dark dichotomy." Most of the city was shrouded in gloom, with only the ring road at the bottom lit by dense, dark red lights—the location of the life-sustaining factories and puppet workshops. Sometimes, Ah Chen thought those lights were like the embers of a wildfire in the wasteland, forever burning, never extinguished, and never warm.

“That’s your world.” The puppet walked to his side. It really made no sound when it walked; Achen didn’t even notice when it arrived.

“No,” Ah Chen said, “that’s the world they allowed us to see.”

The light from the Sun Tower shifted from dim to bright. A cascade of rays poured down from the glass dome, like a silent, golden snowfall, blanketing every inch of the divine realm. Suddenly, all the shadows became clear, taking direction and shape. Ah Chen and the spirit puppet's shadows were projected side-by-side on the workshop floor, one shorter, the other longer.

"The light is coming," the puppet said. Its face was turned towards the window, and the sunlight fell on its cheeks, warm but bright. Suddenly, it reached out and caught a small patch of light that had fallen on the windowsill. "This is my first sunrise."

Ah Chen looked at it. Its fleeting expression—if spirit puppets could be called expressions—reminded him of a long time ago, when he first followed his mentor to climb the outer wall of the divine realm and saw the ruins of that ancient city on the wasteland. At that moment, the artificial sun was just reaching its zenith, its golden light bathing the earth, making all the broken walls seem to have donned new skin, and everything that had died seemed to have come back to life. His mentor stood behind him and said something.

"Remember this feeling. Everything you create in the future must be worthy of this light."

He has now made the first one.

"Close the window," he said.

The puppet withdrew its hand. Its fingertips had lingered in the light for too long, the metal knuckles slightly warm from the sun. But it didn't hesitate, immediately carrying out the command. The light shield closed, and the workshop was once again bathed in the dim, yellowish light of the workroom. Ah Chen walked up to it and began to examine its fingers.

“After tomorrow, I will teach you everything,” he said, “but for now, you need to learn to be quiet first.”

What is quiet?

"It means to stop talking."

"Is stopping speaking a functional pause for me, or a choice?"

Ah Chen raised his head and looked into its eyes. Those blue pupils remained motionless, reflecting his face. He suddenly remembered an old saying from the realm of spirit creation: a spirit puppet is a mirror; if you throw it into darkness, it reflects darkness; if you put it in sunlight, it reflects sunlight. It has no desires of its own, so it will never lie.

“It’s a choice,” Ah Chen said.

The puppet nodded and then fell silent.

Ah Chen continued his examination of its body. He went around to its back and ran his fingers down the curve of the spine to check the precision of the bite between each bone. Suddenly remembering something, he took a small copper piece out of his pocket and handed it to the puppet.

"What is this?" the puppet asked.

“A name tag,” Ah Chen said. “I used to have a dog. It died. The name tag is still with you.”

The puppet looked down at its palm. On the copper plate was a word that had been worn away and blurred: "Ember".

“This is your name now,” Ah Chen said. “Remember, it’s not ‘it,’ it’s ‘you.’”

The spirit puppet—Ember—raised its head, its blue eyes flashing in the dim light. It seemed to want to say something, but in the end it simply closed its hands, holding the copper piece in its palms. It had no body temperature, and the copper piece would soon cool down.

"Lie down now," Ah Chen said. "I'm going to seal your chest cavity."

Jin obediently returned to the stone platform and lay down on its back. Within its open chest, the spiritual energy still pulsated gently, its core emitting a steady, ethereal blue light. Achen picked up the prepared breastplate, bent down, and aligned it with the slot on the edge. He paused.

“There might be some—” he began, then stopped.

Dolls do not feel pain. They do not need such reminders.

“What will happen?” Jin asked.

"It's nothing," Ah Chen said.

He fastened his breastplate. The sound of the clips closing was exceptionally crisp in the workshop, like bones returning to their place. Jin looked down at his chest, which was now smooth and intact, pale as jade, without a single gap.

"It's like being locked up," it said.

"What?"

“The light in my core,” it said, “is trapped inside. I can feel it inside, but I can’t see it.”

Achen didn't reply. He began to pack up his tools, putting the carving knife, copper wire, and spirit network calibrator back in their places one by one. Jin sat up from the stone platform, quietly watching his every move. Its gaze reminded Achen of the starlight on the wasteland—the lingering light of those long-extinguished suns, traveling through millions of years to reach him; though dead, they were still very bright.

"Let's go," Ah Chen said. "I'll take you to get your identity badge."

He walked towards the door. Jin followed silently behind. As they passed the bronze mirror in front of the workshop, Achen subconsciously paused. Two faces were reflected in the mirror, one his, the other Jin's. They were so similar, yet so different.

"What are you looking at?" Jin asked.

“Look at us,” Ah Chen said.

Do we look alike?

“It doesn’t look like it,” Ah Chen said. “It’s just the same face.”

He pushed open the door, and the wind outside, carrying the lingering warmth of the artificial sun, blew in from the ring road. In the distance, towards the wasteland, no one knew what was happening there. All Ah Chen knew was that from below the western horizon, sometimes some dark shadows, belonging to no light source, would rise. They had no shape, no breath, and would simply stand there quietly until they were dispersed by the next burst of light from the artificial sun.

And tonight, there will be even more of those shadows.

“Jin,” he suddenly said.

"yes."

"If one day I asked you to die, would you go?"

"meeting."

"Won't you ask why?"

Do you want me to ask?

Ah Chen didn't answer. He quickened his pace and descended the steps. The wind on the circular path lifted the hem of his robe, like a black wing opening and closing in the dim light. Jin followed two steps behind him, his footsteps so light they were almost imperceptible, like a loyal shadow, unwilling to leave, yet unwilling to approach.

And above the dome, the light inside the Sun Tower began a new cycle of pulse. Three parts bright, two parts dark. The cycle repeated itself, like a vast, indifferent breath.

But everyone knows that this breathing won't continue forever.

Ah Chen closed the workshop door, shutting out the sun. But light still seeped in through the crack in the door, a narrow, golden sliver, like a blunt knife resting on the threshold.

Jin stood by the window, its blue eyes reflecting the light filtering through the crack. Suddenly it said, "You're afraid of me."

Ah Chen's hand paused for a moment. He was putting away his carving knife when the tip veered slightly off course, cutting his thumb as it pierced the leather pouch. A bead of blood seeped out, dark red, like rust that had dried up long ago on a barren wasteland. He didn't wipe it away, but simply continued pushing the knife in.

“I’m not afraid of you,” he said.

"Then why don't you dare let me look into your eyes?" Jin turned around, his two sapphire-like pupils devoid of emotion, only bright. "Every time you talk to me, your gaze falls on my brow, the bridge of my nose, my chin—never on my pupils. You're avoiding me."

Ah Chen's thumb was still bleeding. He finally raised his head and glanced at Jin. It was a very short glance, so short that it was almost not eye contact, but Jin saw it.

“There’s something in your pupils,” Jhin said. “Like a layer of gray. What do you see in my eyes?”

“I saw something clean,” Ah Chen said. “It was so clean. So clean that it made me feel dirty.”

Jin tilted its head. That gesture was designed by Achen to express confusion. But the way it did it now carried a kind of bewilderment that Achen had never expected, a bewilderment that was almost genuine: "What is filth? I have no concept of it."

“Of course you don’t.” Ah Chen put his thumb in his mouth, the rusty taste melting on his tongue. “If you have no desires, you won’t be greedy; if you’re not greedy, you won’t lie; if you don’t lie, you won’t betray. Even the word ‘choice’ is just a syllable to you, not a weight. So you are clean.” He paused, his voice lowering, “like a clean mirror. The mirror only reflects the person looking in it; the mirror itself has nothing.”

Jin remained silent for a long time. Its silence wasn't hesitation, but rather a process. It was processing those things in Chen's words that it couldn't quantify. Then it spoke: "Do you want me to have a choice?"

The question lingered in the workshop air like a leaf falling into a still well. Ah Chen's hand stopped moving. He looked at the wound on his thumb; the bleeding had stopped, leaving a dried, brownish-red line, like half of some ancient rune.

“I don’t know,” he said.

This was the most honest thing he ever said in his life.

Later, they went to the Seventh Boundary Marker. The night before their departure, Jin checked Achen's spirit cores, running his fingers over each one to make sure there were no cracks. His hands appeared translucent under the eerie light of the spirit cores, and the spiritual gold patterns beneath his skin flowed quietly, like unseen rivers deep underground.

“If there’s something outside the boundary marker,” Jhin said, “would you let me block it?”

"meeting."

What if I break?

"If it breaks, it breaks," Ah Chen said. His voice was flat, as flat as the hand he was pressing on the table.

Ember showed no sign of injury—it was never born that way. But when it placed the last spirit core into its pouch, its movements were a beat slower than usual. Then it said, "Even after I shatter, my core will still glow for a while. If you bring me back, I can piece myself back together. It'll just be missing something."

"What's missing?"

“I don’t know,” Jin said. “Just like you don’t know whether you want me to have a choice.”

The artificial sun outside the window was transitioning from a state of "two parts darkness" to "three parts brightness." Light squeezed in through the gaps in the sunshade, like countless burning needles, falling on Jin's back. His shoulder blades showed a pale blue outline in the light, the trace of psionic energy burning beneath his skin. Looking at that outline, Achen suddenly remembered what his mentor had said long ago: Puppet spirits don't die from anything; they only die from exhaustion. But before they're exhausted, they'll keep shining, like something unaware that it is a lamp.

"Put the spirit core away," Achen stood up. "It's almost dawn."

“It’s an artificial sun that’s about to rise,” Jin corrected him.

Ah Chen walked to the door, stopped, and didn't turn around. "Is there a difference?"

Ember stood in the dim light that filled the room, its blue eyes reflecting the spirit cores scattered across the table like a miniature star map. “Perhaps not,” it said, “perhaps. But I don’t need to know the difference. I only need to know—when you leave, I will follow. When you stop, I will stand. Before you shatter, I will shatter first.”

Ah Chen pushed open the door. The wind rushed in, carrying the cold of the wasteland and the saltiness of the deep sea. He stepped out without answering. But in his heart, he uttered a sentence he would never say aloud: This is exactly what I fear most—you even describe "shattered" as a calm statement.

Jin followed closely behind, just a step away. His footsteps were so light they were almost imperceptible, like a moving mirror reflecting the sky where the artificial sun was beginning to brighten.

The seventh boundary marker is at the edge of the wasteland. They're leaving tonight.

The two remained silent for a moment. The wind from the lower level blew in through the cracks, carrying the salty smell of the deep sea—a smell that did not belong to the divine realm, cold, damp, and imbued with some ancient, malevolent vitality.
 
"Chen Du," Wu Jiu suddenly called his name.
 
Shen Du was stunned again. Wu Jiu never called him by his name directly; it always addressed him as "you" or "Creator."
 
"What?"
 
"You just asked if you wanted me to have a choice," Wu Jiu's gray eyes gleamed coldly under the psionic lamp, "I calculated all the possible answers. But I've realized that whatever the answer is, it doesn't matter."
 
"Why?"
 
"Because whether I have a choice or not," Wu Jiu said, his voice still steady and emotionless, but Shen Du heard something he had never heard before in that calmness—perhaps unwavering resolve, perhaps just his own projection—"I will stay by your side. This is not an order, nor a choice. This is a fact."
 
Shen Du stared at it, remaining silent for a long time.
 
The wind rushed in through the cracks in the Ninth Town's Void Tower, ruffling Wu Jiu's black hair. He stood there, under the pale psionic lamps, in the air after the evil spirits had dissipated, like a quiet, sinless mirror.
 
For the first time, Shen Du felt that what was reflected in the mirror might not just be himself.
 
 
 
He patted Wu Jiu on the shoulder. "Let's go," he said, "back to get your hand fixed. Then get ready—we might have to head to the wasteland ahead of schedule."
 
What are you going to do in the wasteland?
