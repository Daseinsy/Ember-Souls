# Grandma

Lin Jing could smell the aroma from three streets away, under the apricot trees outside the wall.

The aroma of hot oil seeping into the batter, mingling with the sizzling of chopped scallions on the hot plate, carried an indescribable sweetness—he had asked his grandmother what it was when he was little. She tapped his forehead with her finger and said, "It's candy. Your father loved sweets when he was a child." Later, he searched the kitchen himself, looking through every jar and bottle, but couldn't find the candy jar. His grandmother used dried radish powder to make the scallions, which, when mixed into the batter, had a slightly sweet taste.

But the taste is just different.

Every Friday evening, the academy's evening classes ended early. Lin Jing bypassed the side gate of Tian Shu Pavilion and slipped out through a gap in the west corner of the wall known only to students. Under the apricot tree stood an old iron plate, its edges chipped in three places and wrapped with copper wire. Grandma sat on a low stool, her apron faded from washing, the front stained with oil and flour. When she saw him, she would wipe her hands on her apron.

"You've lost weight." Grandma always said that, regardless of whether he had gained two pounds last month. She flipped the pancake on the griddle, cracked an egg on it, and used the tip of a spatula to draw a circle as the egg yolk spread. Once the edges were crispy, she folded it into four pieces, stuffed it into an oil paper bag, and handed it over.

"Grandma, I can abstain from grains." Lin Jing took the paper; it was hot to the touch, and he passed it between his hands. "The academy has enough spiritual food."

Grandma wielded her shovel, "The food has a spiritual flavor, but does it have a human flavor?" Behind her was the mottled brickwork of the western wall of the divine realm, the setting sun casting long shadows. Her short figure sat on a low stool, a few strands of silver hair peeking out from under her cloth. Apprentices and servants passing by occasionally bought a coin, the copper coin clinking as it was tossed into the tin box. Grandma never counted the money.

Lin Jing took a bite. The batter was crispy on the outside and soft on the inside, the aroma of scallions wafting up first, followed by the subtle sweetness of the radish powder. As he chewed, he saw his grandmother staring at him, her eyes narrowed, wrinkles piling up at the corners of her eyes like a crumpled old piece of paper.

"Grandma, it'll be soon," Lin Jing mumbled, chewing on a pancake. He had planned to save for another three years—no, two years—after the academy's advancement exam, there would be a ration of spiritual materials. He would sell the rations, and with his wages from his duties, it should be enough for a down payment on a small house on the outskirts of the upper city. "Once I advance to the fourth rank, I can bring you up there. The spiritual barrier in the upper city is strong; evil spirits can't get in."

Grandma slowly scraped the batter off the griddle. "Upper District," she murmured, tapping the spatula against the edge of the griddle twice, "Do the houses in Upper District have yards? I want to plant an apricot tree."

"Yes, definitely," Lin Jing said. "I'll buy you one with a yard, bigger than this one."

Grandma smiled. Her smile was slightly off-center to the right because she had chipped half of her tooth when she was younger. It had healed, but she always had this habitual tendency to smile to one side. "Okay, Grandma will wait."

It was already dusk when Lin Jing walked back to the college that day. He glanced back and saw his grandmother still packing up her stall, placing the iron plate on the cart and tying a low stool to the handle. She bent down to reach for a metal box that had fallen to the ground, bending very low, and only straightened up after pausing, supporting herself on her knees.

Many Fridays later, he still remembered—he remembered the pause when she bent over, the way her silver hair shone faintly in the last rays of the setting sun, and the way her lips turned to the side when she said, "Okay, Grandma will wait."

The change began in late autumn of that year.

First, the pancake tasted different. Lin Jing paused when he took his first bite—the batter tasted sour, as if it had over-fermented, and the scallions were old and tough. He glanced at his grandmother, who was dozing on a low stool, her hands resting on her knees, her fingers trembling slightly.

"Grandma?" He crouched down. Grandma opened her eyes, her gaze drifting past him to the canopy of the apricot tree, as if looking at something very far away. "Nannan," she suddenly said, her voice as soft as a thread, "Are the apricots ripe?"

Apricot trees bear fruit in spring. By late autumn, only bare branches remain.

Lin Jing wrapped up the unfinished pancake and put it in his sleeve. He held his grandmother's arm, and through the old cotton cloth, he could feel the bones. His grandmother's wrist was thinner than before, and her pulse was shallow and rapid, like a stream that was about to dry up.

The academy's medical practitioner examined him and said it was an early sign of spiritual depletion. Lin Jing asked if they had any spiritual medicine, and the practitioner quoted a price. He heard the price twice; the first time, he thought he had misheard, and the second time, he remained silent for a long time.

He increased his shifts from four hours to six a day. At night, he would go to the library to copy spiritual texts, earning three copper coins for each page copied. He would save up the coins and exchange them for the lowest-grade Qi-strengthening Powder. He would mix the powder with water for his grandmother to drink. After drinking it, she would lean against the headboard and gaze out the window, lost in thought. She had been like this lately, looking outside while calling out his father's nickname, and sometimes a string of names he couldn't understand, short and long, as if she were counting people.

"They were all people fleeing from disaster," Aunt Zhao, the neighbor, whispered to him. Aunt Zhao had lived through that era herself. "Your grandmother was only seven years old then, following a group of people from the south to the Land of Gods. Most of them died on the way. Evil spirits chased after them, hiding by day and running by night. More people starved or froze to death than were eaten by the evil spirits. Your grandmother was the youngest in that group, and everyone gave way to her, letting her eat first and hide her at the back. Those who gave way to her, one by one, are all gone."

After finishing her sentence, Aunt Zhao lowered her head, her eyes reddening. "This kind of evil spirit," she said in a low voice, "it doesn't kill people directly. It makes your spirit slowly leak out, like a torn rice sack, leaking until you're completely dried up. Your grandmother saw too many people, too many died before her eyes... their images are hidden in her spirit. For decades, the evil spirit has come to haunt them."

Lin Jing stood outside Aunt Zhao's doorstep. It was completely dark, but the light in his grandmother's room was still on, a small, yellow flame flickering on the window paper. He suddenly remembered the faceless man he had met in his hometown when he was a child; the patterns on his mask shimmered in the firelight. After listening to him, the faceless man left with a single sentence: "Don't expect one person to end everything."

But at that time, all he wanted to do was save his grandmother.

The Qi-Strengthening Powder was no longer effective. Lin Jing increased his duties at the library to eight hours a day. During his classes, his eyelids were so heavy he could barely keep them open, and the spiritual texts seemed to shatter into pieces in his vision. He went to ask Master Gu for help, but Master Gu, busy reviewing documents in his quiet room, didn't even look up and said, "I don't know." He went to the girl who sold talismans in the East Market. After listening to his story, the girl pulled a package from under her stall and handed it to him, saying it was a family recipe, and she didn't know if it would work. He opened it and found a few withered yellow leaves, their edges so brittle they would crumble at the slightest touch.

Later he learned that spiritual evil spirits specifically target those with "gaps" in their spirits. His grandmother's gap was too deep—the bodies of those who died on the escape route decades ago lay there, never given a chance to be buried. The evil spirits simply dug up the soil, causing the old wounds to bleed again. He knew it from the day the pancakes turned sour. He knew his grandmother was leaking, like Aunt Zhao had said, like a rice bag bursting, rice leaking out grain by grain. He tried to plug it with his hands, with the Qi-strengthening powder, with the copper coins from the library and eight hours of his shift. But he was poor, his spiritual level low; he couldn't afford the spiritual barriers of the upper city, nor could he afford high-grade spiritual medicine.

My grandmother passed away in the early hours of the morning.

That night, Lin Jing was copying the last page of the spiritual text in the library when he suddenly felt a hollowness in his chest. The words on the paper seemed to float up, as if drifting on water. He threw down his pen and ran back. As he ran past the side gate of the academy, he bumped into someone. A glimpse of reddish-brown curly hair flashed in the moonlight—Zhou Jin's voice called out something behind him, but he didn't hear it clearly.

When Lin Jing opened the door, Grandma was still in bed. She was lying on her side, facing the window, where only branches remained of the apricot tree. Her eyes were closed, and her lips were slightly parted, as if she were saying something but hadn't finished. Lin Jing squatted down in front of the bed and put his ear close. All he could hear was her breathing, growing weaker and weaker until finally, like a string snapping, she let out a soft "hum" and then it was gone.

He touched his grandmother's hand. Her fingers were trembling. He clenched his fist, but couldn't. Her hand was still warm, but her fingertips were cold. He tucked her arm, which was sticking out of the blanket, back in and tucked the blanket in, just as she used to tuck him in. Then he sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, so long that the window paper turned from black to gray, and from gray to white.

As the morning light streamed in, he glanced down and saw half a piece of oiled paper sticking out of his sleeve. It was the oiled paper from the pancake he'd made yesterday evening—he'd clutched it in his sleeve the whole way there, and before leaving, he'd pulled it out to show his grandmother, saying, "Grandma, I'm almost saved enough for a house in the city center. A house with a yard, and we'll plant apricot trees."

Grandma took the oil paper, slowly running her fingers along its surface, smiled, and her lips curled to the right. "Okay," she said, "Grandma will wait."

Lin Jing pulled the oil paper out of his sleeve, folded it, and put it back in. He sat in the morning light, without crying. The apricot tree outside the window swayed its withered branches in the morning breeze, as if beckoning.

He suddenly wondered if she had ever imagined that when she was young—at seven years old, she had run from the south to the Kingdom of God and crawled out of a pile of dead bodies—she would one day be selling pancakes under an apricot tree, with a grandson coming to eat every Friday and saying, "It'll be ready soon."

She waited. She kept waiting.

Lin Jing pressed his forehead against the edge of the bed, the coolness of the wood seeping into his skin. He remembered the lines on the Faceless Man's mask, lines that seemed to be both crying and laughing, and the eyes within those ghostly flames. The Faceless Man had said not to expect one person to end everything. But what he hadn't understood at the time was—not expecting one person to end everything didn't mean you had to do nothing.

Even after it's all done, some things will still happen.

A morning breeze slipped in through the cracks in the window, carrying the scent of withered autumn grass. Lin Jing looked up, stood up, and went to the kitchen to boil a kettle of water. Grandma's teacup was still on the table, a few unopened tea leaves sinking to the bottom. He poured in the hot water, the tea leaves rising and then sinking again. Steam rose, carrying a slightly astringent aroma of tea. It wasn't the smell of pancakes. Never had it. But it was enough. He placed the cup on Grandma's bedside table. Outside the window, a bird landed on the apricot tree and fluttered its wings.
