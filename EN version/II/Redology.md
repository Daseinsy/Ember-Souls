#  Redology

This question could almost be sent straight to the grave of "Redology" and let it tumble around on its own.

The vast majority of literature professors and teachers only read the "standard answer" and "academic achievements"; a very small number of them do read the deepest meaning, but they dare not say it, or no one would believe them if they did.

These people aren't reading "Dream of the Red Chamber," they're reading about "making a living."
They need to publish papers, apply for professional titles, and give lectures—and "Dream of the Red Chamber" is the best "academic goldmine." You can dig a little bit and write dozens of papers.

Indeed, there are a very small number of scholars who, when reading "Dream of the Red Chamber," truly "immerse themselves in it."

They don't engage in textual research, don't play with hidden meanings, and don't attach ideological labels.
They read it word by word, reading out Cao Xueqin's "unbearable" feelings, his "disillusionment," and the "compassion" he poured into each character.

But they face a huge dilemma:

If we were to tell students that "Jia Baoyu is a nihilist destined to fail" or that "Dream of the Red Chamber is actually saying that all efforts are futile,"...

It's not that they don't understand, it's that they dare not (or cannot) lay bare that coldest, darkest, and most nihilistic meaning on the podium.

If you absolutely had to sum up the deepest meaning of "Dream of the Red Chamber" in one sentence, it would probably be:

"This world is meaningless; all love, hate, fame, fortune, and glory will eventually return to 'a vast expanse of white, truly clean'; but even so, you can still shed a tear for those real emotions in 'meaninglessness'—this is the only thing worth doing."

Therefore, the vast majority of professors chose to "castrate" "Dream of the Red Chamber"—smoothing out its rebellious spirit, warming up its coldness, and packaging it into a "positive energy" classic that is "uplifting," "criticizing feudalism," and "praising love," before stuffing it into the teaching syllabus and making generations of students memorize the "standard answers" for exams.

Cao Xueqin wrote in his book, "Everyone says the author is crazy, but who can understand the taste within?" He had long foreseen this scene: more than two hundred years later, there will still be a group of "clever people" who will carefully wrap up "that taste", attach various academic labels, and then put it in a glass case for exhibition, pretending that they have "understood" it.

Those who have truly "understood" it often remain silent, only sighing softly, closing the book, turning off the light, and going to sleep when they reread "The crane's shadow crosses the cold pond, the cold moon buries the flower's soul" late at night.
