She was too weak to open her eyes at birth, yet she was already experiencing the sharp edge of ice.

The air was as cold as a knife, cutting through her lungs, but the pain was vivid, proving she was alive. She tried to move, and the scales peeled away from the ice, making a tiny cracking sound, like a tree branch breaking in the deepest part of winter.

She didn't know why she was lying there, or who she was. She only felt empty inside, as if countless voices had once called out to her, but now she was all alone, and even the echoes were scarce.

She crawled out of the crevasse. The bright sunlight shone down, almost pinning her to the spot. Her body was pitifully small, her scales dull, and she didn't even have the strength to lift her head. But hunger and cold forced her forward, inch by inch, crawling over the frozen earth and withered grass.

Then, she heard the panting of a wild beast.

A grey-backed jackal emerged from behind a rock, its yellow eyes locking onto her, drool dripping from its mouth. She wanted to run away, but her body felt like it was made of lead; she could only raise her head slightly, flick her tongue, and hiss threateningly—a sound so weak that she could barely hear it herself.

"Father! There's a little snake over there!"

A child's voice suddenly rang out. Immediately followed by heavy, rapid footsteps. A man's figure stood between her and the jackal, a spear thrusting out, slicing through the air. The jackal growled, took a few steps back, staring resentfully, before finally turning and running into the woods.

"It's alright now." The man panted as he turned to look at her. He was dressed in coarse linen clothes, his face etched with the marks of time, but his eyes were gentle.

"It's so small." The child squatted down; he was a boy, about seven or eight years old, with rosy cheeks. "Is it hurt?"

"It looks like it's frozen." The man's wife came over, carrying a wild rabbit. She squatted down to look at it. "Don't touch it, snakes bite."

“It doesn’t bite me.” The boy stretched out his finger, then pulled it back, and whispered, “It can’t move.”

She truly couldn't move. Even the slightest movement of her nostrils became difficult. But she remembered the warmth of the child's palm, a finger's width away, like a small flame.

She was taken back to the hunter's hut. It had a thatched roof and earthen walls, and a campfire burned inside. The woman wrapped her in an old cloth and placed her by the fire. The boy brought a bowl of warm water, dipped a blade of grass in it, and dripped it to her lips. She instinctively opened her mouth; the water moistened her throat, bringing a breath of life.

"Is it a blue snake?" the boy asked.

"What green snake?" The father was skinning a rabbit with a knife, not even looking up.

"The elder said that long ago, a giant snake, a deity, lived in the mountains. Later, they all died, except for one, which was frozen in ice." The boy's eyes sparkled. "Could it be that one?"

"Don't talk nonsense," the mother scolded gently. "There are no gods."

“But it came out of the ice…” the boy tried to argue.

"Eat." His mother handed him the roasted rabbit meat, interrupting him.

She curled up by the fire, slowly regaining her body temperature. She neither denied nor admitted it, because she herself wasn't sure. She only remembered that long, long ago, she seemed to have many of her kind, shedding their skin together in the moonlight and swimming through moss in the rain. But later, the world grew colder and quieter, and then, she was left alone, placed in ice by someone, waiting for a dawn she didn't know.

Those memories were like they were frozen in time, hazy and indistinct. All she knew was that from now on, this family would be her only place to live.

Later, she grew up. Her body grew from the thickness of a finger to the thickness of an arm, until even an adult man couldn't encircle her. She no longer needed to hide by the fire, but she still loved to be near that warm light. The hunter's son—that boy—had grown into a young man and married a woman from the neighboring village. The old man, his hair gray, often sat in front of his house, gazing at the distant mountains, sometimes for half a day at a time.

She was revered as the tribe's totem. It all began with a flash flood; she sensed it beforehand, letting out a sharp cry that roused the sleeping people. The flood destroyed half the village, but no one perished. The elder knelt tremblingly before her, saying, "The Azure Serpent has appeared." From then on, her image was carved on wooden pillars, and her name was written into prayers. Every spring, people dance around bonfires, praying to her for a bountiful harvest and peace.

She didn't consider herself a god. She had simply lived a little longer, long enough to witness the entirety of a person's life. She watched the hunter grow old, watched him cough, bend over, and finally, on some winter night, quietly cease breathing. His wife had gone before him, and his son wept beside him. She sat cross-legged on the roof beam, letting out low sobs, like the wind blowing through an empty valley. No one heard her.

Later, the boy grew old. His son and grandson sat around the fire, telling stories of their ancestors, of the snake that emerged from the ice. As she listened, she would occasionally recall that afternoon many years ago, the sun was cold, she was nearly mauled to death by jackals, and a child's voice pierced the silence: "Father! There's a little snake over there!"

Back then, she was just a little snake, ignored by everyone. She didn't know what loneliness, death, or endless years were. All she knew was that the child's palm, only a finger's width away, was warm.

Generation after generation grows old and dies. Her body grows larger and larger, but her heart grows smaller and smaller, until it can only hold the original bonfire.

The signs of ascension came without warning.

That night, she perched on an ancient tree on the mountain behind the tribe, and suddenly her scales shimmered with a faint golden light, as if countless tiny tongues of fire were licking her from within. The spiritual energy of heaven and earth began to gather towards her; the wind stopped, the stars hung low, and the entire mountain forest trembled slightly. She knew that this was a sign of becoming a god, the moment she had waited for for countless years.

But at that very moment, the southeastern horizon was dyed a dark red. Not the glow of dawn, but fire. Thick smoke billowed, like black serpents, constricting the horizon. Faint cries drifted in the wind, intermittent and torn to pieces by the distance.

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the golden light had faded. She raised her head and soared into the air towards the flames.

By the time she arrived, the tribe was gone.

The thatched roofs were torn off, the mud walls collapsed, and several totem poles lay askew in the scorched earth, their snake patterns blurred by the fire. Survivors gathered in small groups in the open space, their bodies covered in wounds, their eyes vacant, as if their souls had been ripped away. Some searched through the ruins, calling out the names of their loved ones. Their cries fell silent in the ashes.

When she landed, her massive body made the ground sag. People saw her and were first filled with fear, then some knelt down, their voices trembling as they cried out, "Lord Azure Serpent...Lord Azure Serpent has returned..."

She ignored them. She swam straight to the old locust tree in the east of the village. There lay a person under the tree, a girl, about seven or eight years old, her face covered in soot, with a deep wound on her chest, the blood soaking into the soil beneath her.

She recognized her. She was the last child of that hunter's family, and her name was Sui.

Sui's breathing was shallow, her eyelids half-open. When she saw her, her lips moved, but no sound came out. She lowered her head, and Nobuko gently touched the girl's fingers.

“Snake… Sister…” Sui’s voice seemed to drift from afar, “Mother said… you will protect us…”

A low whimper escaped her throat. She couldn't speak, at least not yet; she couldn't respond with a human voice. She could only curl up, holding the girl close, just like many, many years ago, by that campfire, when a child fed her water with blades of grass.

"It hurts so much..." Sui murmured, "I...I miss Father...Father went to war...He said he would come back...But I can't wait for him anymore..."

The snake's tongue trembled slightly.

An old woman staggered over, hugged Sui, and cried so hard she could barely breathe: "Sui'er, Sui'er, hold on! Your father will be back soon. He'll be back after winning the battle..."

"Grandma, I want to tell Dad..." Sui's voice trailed off, "I want to say that I'm not afraid... I'm not afraid of pain... I just... miss him a little..."

The old woman wailed loudly. Several villagers around her turned away, wiping their eyes with their sleeves. A middle-aged man clenched his fists and said in a hoarse voice, "Whose war is this anyway! We're all human beings, born of parents, how did it turn out like this! No food, no medicine, not even a way to survive!"

"Stop talking..." The woman beside him pulled him back, "Don't say these things in front of the children."

"Not talk? If you don't talk, how can she come back to life?" The man squatted down, buried his head in his hands, and his shoulders trembled violently. "I watched my mother starve to death by the roadside, without even a straw mat... She was calling my name at the very end... What use am I? I couldn't even get her a bowl of porridge!"

The wind blew across the ruins, swirling up black ash like a silent snowfall.

The snake tightened its coils around the girl's head. She could feel the girl's body heat slowly fading, like sand slipping through her fingers. She had seen generations pass away like this, from hunters to their sons, and then to their grandsons. She thought she was used to it, but now, that familiar, dull pain gripped her again.

Within her, the power that had accumulated before her ascension was still surging. The golden light shone again, growing ever brighter. The chance to become a god was fleeting; if missed, who knew how many more years she would have to wait? Yet, her heart was being pulled down by another force, plummeting towards this scorched earth.

She remembered many voices. Those who died in the war, the words left unspoken, the last "I miss you" carried away by the wind. They could not convey their voices, could not reach the people they longed to reach. And she, a snake who had lived so long and was revered as a deity, could only watch helplessly, unable to offer even a word of comfort.

"Sister Snake..." Sui spoke again, her voice barely audible, "If... if I could become a star... Father could look up and see me... I could tell him I don't blame him... I miss him so much..."

The girl stopped breathing.

The old woman's crying stopped abruptly, as if something had abruptly cut it off. She opened her mouth but couldn't make a sound, only shaking the child in her arms incessantly, as if that would wake her up.

The snake raised its head. There were no stars in the night sky, only smoke and firelight, layer upon layer, like a giant hand covering the sky.

Then, she did something.

She slowly released her grip on the tassel, turning her head south, towards the still-burning battlefield. A burst of golden light erupted from within her, not rising upwards, but spreading outwards. The light was so dazzling that people had to close their eyes. Within the light, an extremely thin and bright ray rose from the top of her head, like a stretched thread, piercing straight towards the heavens.

She sent a part of her soul into the sky.

The azure star appeared at the highest point, above the smoke and dust, above the firelight, shining silently. Unlike other stars, it was not distant and cold; it carried a warm tremor, as if someone were gently breathing in the dome.

A child on the ground spotted it first, pointing to the sky and shouting, "Stars! Blue stars!"

People looked up. The star was bright, so blue it looked like it had been pulled from the deep sea, or like someone had frozen a tear in the sky.

The old woman slowly stopped crying. She looked at the star, then suddenly picked up Sui'er and whispered with all her might, "Sui'er...can you hear me...your father will see...he will definitely see..."

At that moment, the snake felt as if a part of her soul had truly heard the old woman's words. The voice traveled along some invisible path, heading towards someone on a distant battlefield. She even saw the man— Sui's father—standing amidst a pile of corpses, clutching a chipped blade and covered in blood. He suddenly raised his head, looked up at the sky, his lips moved as if in response, and then continued charging forward.

The snake's body remained in place. She was not dead, but no longer whole. A part of her soul separated and transformed into that star. From then on, when people sincerely made kind wishes to it, those voices could transcend time and space to reach where they wished to go.

Her body fell heavily to the ground, and the golden light on her scales vanished completely. She was no longer the great serpent on the verge of becoming a god; she had simply returned to being a serpent, weary, broken, yet still alive.

Silence fell. The wind died down, but the fire continued to burn.

The man who had been crouching on the ground slowly stood up, walked to her, and asked softly, "Lady Azure Serpent... are you still able to stand up?"

She didn't answer. She simply turned her gaze to the far north. There, a darkness deeper than any human civil war was gathering, slowly creeping in like a tide.

And she had to stand up.

The darkness in the north came sooner than expected.

At first, it was just a blurry gray shadow on the horizon, like clouds hanging low before a storm. But overnight, that gray shadow spread into an endless black tide. The earth trembled, as if a million feet were trampling across it simultaneously, and even distant rocks began to loosen and tumble down. A stench filled the air, like rotting flesh mixed with rust, choking one's eyes.

She perched atop the highest ridge to the north of the tribe, her body blotting out the sun, her scales gleaming with a bluish-black light. But when she gazed upon that dark tide, for the first time she felt that her immense size was nothing more than a flimsy fence, while across from her lay the entire sea.

"My lord! The monsters have crossed the Broken River!" A scout scrambled up the ridge, his face covered in blood. "A dark mass, countless in number! Their vanguard has already devoured two villages!"

Below the ridge, people were building walls with wood and stones. Old people, women, children, even teenagers barely able to hold spears, all poured out of the houses. No one spoke; only heavy breathing and the dull thud of tools striking stone filled the air. Fear, like a transparent layer of ice, froze everyone inside.

She slowly turned her head, flicked her tongue at the scout, signaling him to retreat. Then she raised her head and let out a long, deep whistle towards the crowd below.

That wasn't a threat, it was a call.

People looked up. In that instant, they seemed to hear an ancient promise in the howling sound—as if their ancestors had been summoned in the same way, gathered around the campfire, gazing up at the same figure.

"Lord Azure Serpent says, take the old and children into the cave!" a man draped in animal skins shouted. He was the tribe's best hunter, named Tiemu, and he held a gleaming stone axe in his hand. "Anyone who can wield a knife, follow me!"

“I’m not leaving.” An elderly woman with white hair stepped out from the crowd, clutching a sharpened wooden stick in her hand. “My old arms and legs can’t walk anymore. You go and fight, I’ll carry the stones for you.”

"Grandma!" A young woman pulled her, "You'll only be..."

"What will happen? Only death?" The old woman laughed, revealing her missing front tooth. "I've lived for over seventy years and witnessed three wars, and I couldn't escape any of them. This time, I'll accompany Lord Azure Serpent, so at least I'll die a more spectacular death."

Temu opened his mouth, but ultimately did not try to persuade him further.

Three days later, the monster arrived.

The first impact occurred at dusk. The setting sun dyed the earth blood-red, and the horde of monsters crashed against the mountain ridge like a black wave. Some resembled giant wolves but had three eyes; some were neither human nor non-human, walking on all fours with their backs bulging high; most were just blurry black shadows, leaving withered vegetation and blackened soil in their wake.

The serpent didn't wait for them to get close. She leaped down from the ridge, her massive body crashing into the horde of monsters like a small mountain collapsing, instantly crushing hundreds of them. Her tail swept across, whipping up a stench that turned the nearest giant wolves into a bloody pulp. Poisonous mist spewed from her mouth, hissing and melting wherever it touched, as if splashed with acid.

"kill--!"

Human warriors charged out from both flanks. Ironwood led the charge, swinging his stone axe and cleaving open a monster's head. Black blood splattered across his face, and he grinned, "Is that all you've got? Come on, try again!"

However, there were too many monsters. One wave crashed down only to be replaced by another, seemingly endless. On the first night, humanity held the ridge with her and the terrain. On the second night, a breach was torn in the southern defenses, and more than a dozen warriors fell at the gap. On the third night, a giant scorpion-like monster pierced her left abdomen with its stinger, her dark blue scales bursting open, blood gushing forth.

The blood fell to the ground, blooming into tiny blue flowers, which withered in an instant.

She trembled with pain, but did not retreat an inch. She curled up, using her body to block the breach, letting the demon's claws and teeth fall on her. Her blood stained the entire land, flowing from the mountain ridge to the stream at the foot of the mountain, turning the water a pale blue.

"My lady! I can't hold on any longer!" A young man covered in wounds stumbled to her side, crying out, "The west side is breached too! Brothers... we've almost all fought our way through!"

She lowered her head and looked at the young man. He looked no more than seventeen or eighteen years old, with a childish face and a deep, bone-revealing wound on his arm, from which blood mixed with mud dripped.

"Are you scared?" she suddenly asked.

The young man was stunned. He looked up at her, seemingly unable to believe that this giant snake could speak human language.

"Are you scared?"

"I...I'm scared." The young man's lips trembled, and tears rolled down his cheeks. "I'm afraid of dying. But I don't want to run away."

She was silent for a moment, then exhaled softly, like a sigh.

“Go and call the people who are still alive,” she said. “I have something to say.”

A campfire was lit in the middle of the night. The flame was small, like a bean in the darkness, and could be blown out by the wind at any moment. People surrounded her, each covered in blood and grime, yet their faces bore an almost numb stubbornness.

She lingered outside the firelight, half her body submerged in darkness, only her eyes like two eerie blue lamps.

“We can’t win in a head-on fight.” She spoke, her voice low and deep, as if it came from underground. “If this continues, there won’t be any survivors here within three days.”

No one spoke. Tiemu wiped the stone axe with his sleeve, striking it forcefully, again and again.

“I’m going to fake a surrender,” she said.

Temu suddenly looked up: "What?"

“I will feign surrender to the monsters, infiltrate their lair, and find an opportunity to dismantle them from within.” Her voice was so calm that it didn’t sound like she was talking about a matter of life and death. “But I need people to go with me. Form a suicide squad and go with me. This journey will be one of certain death.”

"My lord!" A one-armed man knelt down. "You can't go! If you go, we..."

“If I don’t go, you won’t even have tomorrow,” she interrupted him. “I’ve lived too long and seen too many people die. This time, I want to do it differently.”

“Then… count me in.” A thin man stood up from the crowd. He didn’t look strong, and his face even had a somewhat frail appearance, but his eyes were surprisingly bright. His name was Sam.

Timur turned to look at him: "Sam? You can't even hold a knife properly, are you going to your death?"

“I’m going too.” The seventeen or eighteen-year-old boy stood up, his arm still bleeding from a wound. “Anyway, we’ll just die if we stay.”

“Me too,” the one-armed man said.

"I."

"Count me in."

One by one, people stepped forward. The firelight danced on their faces, reflecting an indescribable radiance. She suddenly remembered that night many, many years ago, when the hunter's child squatted by the fire, feeding her water with blades of grass, his eyes just as bright.

“Okay,” she said.

She turned to Sam, her gaze lingering on his face for a moment. This man, who seemed least like a warrior, stood ramrod straight, his lips pressed tightly together, as if he had already made a decision. She didn't know what was churning within him, nor did she know that his wife and children were waiting for him to return. She only saw in him that small flame.

“We’ll set off at dawn tomorrow,” she said.

The night deepened. The campfire grew weaker, like someone slowly closing their eyes. She slowly raised her head and looked up at the azure star in the sky. It was still shining, all alone, yet stubbornly shining.

Her blood was still flowing.

The suicide squad set off before dawn.

Sam walked in the middle of the group, clutching a short knife. It was Timur's knife; the handle was wrapped with blood-soaked strips of cloth, making it sticky in his hand. He wasn't watching where he was going, just following the footsteps of the person in front of him, trudging through the muddy ground. The wind blew from the north, carrying that lingering stench, like a damp cloth covering his face.

His thoughts drifted back to that night.

That was the night before he left home. His wife was mending his clothes under the lamp, sewing slowly, stitch by stitch. The child was already asleep, his small body curled up on the straw mat, his breathing as light as the wind. He sat on the threshold, gazing at the dark sky beyond the courtyard wall, unable to utter a single word.

"Are you hiding something from me?" The wife didn't look up, her voice very soft, as if afraid of waking the child.

“No,” he said.

“Every time you lie, you turn your face away.” His wife put down her needlework, walked over to him, and sat down. Her shoulder rested against his, its touch slightly warm. “You’re going to war, aren’t you?”

He didn't say anything.

“I know, many men in the village have gone,” his wife said, her voice still soft. “I won’t stop you. But you have to promise me you’ll come back.”

“I… will try,” he said.

“It’s not just about trying.” His wife turned to look at him, her eyes glistening in the moonlight. “It’s a must. You are our world. If the world falls, how will we live?”

He opened his mouth, but ultimately didn't dare look her in the eye. He was afraid that if he opened his mouth, tears would fall.

At that time, he had no idea that he would join the Expendables. He also had no idea that once he left, he would never be able to return.

At this moment, he walked in the procession, the moonlight of that night overlapping with his wife's eyes, like a dull knife, cutting into his heart again and again. He remembered the child's laughter, the way the child toddled towards him when he first called "Daddy," and the jujube tree in the yard, which was full of bright red dates every autumn.

"Sam, stay close," someone whispered to him from ahead.

He snapped out of his daze and nodded.

The tree branches twisted like demonic claws, and the ground was covered in a viscous black liquid that made a nauseating sound when stepped on. The surroundings were eerily quiet, save for the sound of their footsteps and their heavy breathing.

"There's a sentry ahead." The Serpent's voice came from ahead, extremely low, as if gliding along the ground. "Three people per team, go around. Sam, you come with me."

"Are you scared?" she suddenly asked.

He looked up at her in surprise. He had always thought that someone like her would not be afraid.

“I’ve lived a long time and seen a lot of death.” Her voice was low, as if she were talking to herself. “But every time, I’m still afraid. Afraid that I’m not fast enough, afraid that I’m not strong enough, afraid that I can’t save the people I want to save.”

"Then why do you still want to go?"

She paused for a moment, then said, "Because I'm afraid, I have to go even more."

Sam didn't say anything more. He gripped the knife tightly, his knuckles turning white.

They bypassed the monsters' outposts and slipped into the depths of a fissure in the earth. It was the entrance to the monsters' lair, and black mist, carrying a stench and intense heat, surged up from the ground. They slid into the fissure one by one, Sam being the last to go down. Just as his feet were about to leave the ground, a sharp shriek suddenly came from afar, followed by the roars of countless monsters—their actions had been discovered.

"Hurry!" Temu roared from below.

Sam jumped down, landing on the soft, decaying soil. He heard the sound of rocks rolling behind him; the monster pursuers were closing in.

"Someone has to lure them away, or we'll all die here," Temu said, panting, looking at the others.

No one spoke.

Sam looked up and saw the serpent using its body to hold the rock above the crack closed. Her blood seeped from her old wound and dripped onto his face, warm.

“I’ll go,” he said.

Everyone looked at him. Timur's eyes widened: "Sam, you—"

“I’ll go,” he repeated, his voice not loud but unusually clear. “I’m the fastest runner in our village. I… I can lead them away. You guys keep going.”

“You will die,” the one-armed man said.

“I know.” Sam suddenly smiled, a smile that was somewhat painful, like a cry. “But if I don’t go, we’ll all die. Someone has to go. That person can be me.”

He remembered his wife's words: "If the sky falls, how will we live?" But what he was thinking at that moment was that if the sky were to fall, someone had to hold it up first. Even if it was just for a little while, even if it was just for a short while.

He turned and crawled out of the crack.

Timur called out from behind him, "Sam! Is there anything else you want to take back with you?"

Sam paused. His back was slightly hunched, as if he were carrying something unseen.

“Tell my wife… just say…” He sniffed, his voice trembling slightly, “that I’m sorry to her. Tell her… to live well. To raise the child. And… and also, I miss her so much. So, so much.”

After saying that, he climbed out without looking back.

The monsters outside had already surrounded them. Sam took a deep breath, then with all his might, ran away from the crevice. He ran as fast as a deer being chased by a hunter. One winter, she lingered beside a crumbling city wall. The city had run out of food for three days, and monsters surrounded it from the outside. The general guarding the city ordered the old and wounded soldiers to be driven outside the walls, saying it would conserve food. Cries and curses erupted from the crowd. A white-haired man grabbed the young general's leg, saying, "My son has served you for three years! He took an arrow for you! You can't do this to me!" The general glanced down at him and simply said, "Take him out."

She was right below the city wall. Those people were pushed into the snow, their breath quickly dissipating in the cold wind. The monster didn't kill them immediately, but merely circled them from a distance, as if toying with them. Those people cried and screamed all night. At dawn, the sound ceased.

The general stood atop the city wall, his face ashen, yet he never gave the order to open the gates. It wasn't until she slowly rose from the snow, shielding the group of old men and wounded soldiers with her body, that she faced the approaching demons alone. She fought that battle for a day and a night, rescuing half the people. But her heart felt as if it had been swept by the wind and snow outside the city, chilled to the bone.

Later, she began to intentionally observe those inconspicuous people.

The woman tending the fire broke her own rations in half, handing one half to a wounded stranger. The one-armed supply soldier carried the wounded back and forth on the front lines, exhausting himself to the point of vomiting blood. And then there were the children , thin as sticks, who would slip the half-eaten bread they had hidden into the hands of even younger children. They had no names, they would not be written into any history books. They could not even be considered brave, for they too felt fear, they too flinched, and they too cried secretly at night.

But they did it anyway.

Once, she shrank to a small size and coiled herself beside a dry well. A boy of about ten years old ran over, squatted down in front of her, and took out a small wooden bowl from his pocket, half-filled with murky water. "Little snake, would you like some water?" he said. She didn't move. The boy gently put down the wooden bowl, turned around, and ran away.

She stared at the half-bowl of water for a long time.

At that moment, the fire in her heart that was about to go out suddenly jumped a little again.

She thought that perhaps a flame can only ever be confined to a small space. It cannot illuminate the entire darkness, nor burn away the weeds covering the ground. It can only light up a small circle around itself, and warm only the few people closest to it. But as long as there are still people burning with passion, this world is still worth believing in.

Perhaps the future will be better. Perhaps it will be worse. Perhaps one day, even this tiny flame will be blown out by the wind. But that's a matter for the future. Right now, they're still burning. The firewood will run out, the wind will pick up, but at this moment, the firelight reflected in their eyes is warm.

She looked up and saw the blue star still hanging in the sky, shining quietly, as if reminding her: Once upon a time, a person used his short life to gain a courage that he would never regret.

“I can’t bear to see them cry anymore,” she whispered.

Having said that, she slowly rose and walked down the mountain. The wind chased after her, stretching her shadow long and light. In the distant camp, new torches were lit. One, two, three. It was as if someone was secretly lighting eyes in the darkness.

They are tiny. But they are burning.
The plan to feign surrender was fraught with bloodshed from the very beginning.

The monsters aren't stupid. They've inhabited this continent for too long and have long since learned to distinguish truth from falsehood. They don't trust her, and they trust humans even less. She must offer a testament to her loyalty—proof enough to convince them that she has broken with humanity.

The lightest punishment is killing one person.

The monster leader was a massive, churning black shadow, like a nightmare squeezed from the depths of the earth. It had no fixed form, only countless eyes opening and closing in the darkness. It had no mouth, yet its voice came from all directions, carrying the scorching heat of magma.

"Azure Serpent, you are the last god in this world. How can you expect us to believe in you?"

"I'd give my life for it," she said.

"Your life will be ours sooner or later." The voice chuckled, like a rumble when the earth splits. "Not enough."

“Then let’s add another one,” she said.

"Whose life?"

"You choose."

The monster leader fell silent. Countless eyes narrowed simultaneously, as if scrutinizing her.

"I want you to kill someone with your own hands," it finally said. "A warrior. A member of a suicide squad."

She didn't move.

"What, you're reluctant to part with her?" The voice drew closer, and black energy coiled around her body like tentacles. "Or were you just faking your surrender from the beginning?"

“I’ll kill,” she said.

That night, she returned to the camp.

The suicide squad already knew the news. No one spoke. They sat around the fire, the firelight illuminating their tired but peaceful faces.

Temu looked at her and asked, "Who should I choose?"

“It said, a warrior,” she said softly, as if talking about something unrelated to herself, “must be killed by my own hands. It can’t be from illness, it can’t be an accident. It must be in front of the monsters, in their presence.”

“I’ll go.” A one-armed man stood up. His name was Chi Yan, and he was among the first to join the suicide squad. He looked at her and smiled. “I was saved from a pile of corpses. If I can use this to fool those beasts, it’s worth it.”

“You’re going to die a horrible death,” she said.

“I know.” Chi Yan touched his empty sleeve. “In my current state, I can’t kill many on the battlefield. But this is something I can do.”

"Chiyan, you still have an elderly mother at home," someone whispered.

“My mother died long ago.” Chi Yan shook his head. “She died last winter. I didn’t even get to see her one last time. Before she left, she asked someone to tell me not to look back. I’ve always remembered that.”

He walked up to her and looked up. The firelight illuminated his rough, dark face, which showed no fear, only an almost stubborn calm.

"Lord Azure Serpent, I have no regrets," he said.

She lowered her head and memorized the face with her eyes. Like the faces she had memorized—the hunter's face, the ear of grain, Sam's face. Each one was a knife, but she had to catch them.

Of all the warriors, only one knew the truth. The others were only told that the risk of death was very high, so that the play would become real and make the monsters really believe that the Azure Serpent had betrayed humanity.

The next day, she personally killed Red Rock in front of the monster.

She plunged her venomous fangs into his chest. His body quickly stiffened and turned cold, his skin turning bluish-purple, before he collapsed onto the black earth. Throughout the ordeal, she heard the whispers and maniacal laughter of the monsters, who stood by like a flock of vultures. She heard Red Rock cough up blood with his last breath, then say something that was carried away by the wind. No one heard it clearly.

Only she knew.

He said, "Kill a few more for me."

Blood dripped from the corner of her mouth. She trembled, but she didn't let herself fall. In front of the monster, she took Crimson Rock's knife from beside his corpse and tucked it under the scales around her neck.

“Is that enough?” she asked.

The monster leader slowly opened its countless eyes, revealing a satisfied light.

That's enough.

When the news reached the human camp, it caused an uproar.

"She killed Red Rock! She sided with the demons!"

"I knew it! She's a beast! A cold-blooded wretch!"

"We've been sold out! We've all been sold out!"

Anger spread like wildfire. People smashed her totems and toppled the altars dedicated to her. Some wept and cursed, others threw stones in her direction. Those who had once knelt before her now uttered the most venomous words, hurling them at her like knives.

Temu stood silently outside the crowd. He said nothing. He knew the truth, but he couldn't speak of it. Because the plan had only just begun.

The remaining members of the suicide squad fell silent. Some began to have doubts, some secretly wept at night, and some simply sharpened their knives again and again, as if waiting for something.

Only that seventeen or eighteen-year-old boy stayed with Tie Mu. His name was Qing Que, and although the wound on his arm had scabbed over, his eyes were still as bright as before.

"Brother Tiemu, do you believe in Lord Cangshe?" he asked.

"I believe you," Temu said.

"Why?"

Temu gazed at the dark clouds in the distance and said, "Because she is not a god. She had more reason to run than any of us, but she didn't. She stayed."

Qingque nodded, as if encouraging herself: "I believe her. She won't really kill Chiyan. She must have a way."

“She really killed him,” Temu said, his voice deep. “You must remember, Chiyan was also a warrior. He and she both risked their lives.”

Qingque's eyes reddened, but he didn't let the tears fall.

Seven days later, deep within the demon's lair, the Azure Serpent unleashed the power that had accumulated within it for a thousand years.

It was a grand death.

A burst of blue light erupted from the earth, as if someone had lifted a star from the earth's core. The monster's lair was torn to shreds, and countless dark shadows screamed and vanished in the light, like snow meeting a raging fire. The earth trembled all night, mountains crumbled, and rivers changed course. The soaring blue light could be seen from a thousand miles away, like a farewell, yet also like a rebirth.

On the city walls, people stared in terror at the light. Some thought it was a calamity, others knelt in prayer. Only Tiemu and Qingque stood in the wind, tears streaming silently down their faces.

“She succeeded,” Qingque said, her voice trembling.

"Yes." Temu looked up at the soaring blue light. "She... succeeded."

The demon army, without a leader, descended into chaos. Humanity seized the opportunity to launch a general offensive, advancing northwards, reclaiming lost territory, and driving the remaining demons off the continent. The war ended; grass grew back on the plains, and the rivers became clear once more. People began to rebuild their homes, burying the dead and the shattered years.

Her body, along with Red Rock, Sam, and countless others who sacrificed themselves, is scattered across every inch of this land.

Some say she's dead. Some say she's not. Some say she turned to ashes and melted into the wind. But no one has ever seen her again.

Only that one azure star still shines in the night sky. From that night on, it has never gone out. It hangs high in the heavens, lonely and gentle, as if guarding every tiny flame on earth.

Will your familiar melody still echo in the distance?

A breeze blew across the wilderness, carrying the scents of campfires and wildflowers. Lights floated along the riverbank, one after another, stretching towards the horizon. Someone stood on the bank, hands clasped together, whispering a wish towards the blue star. That wish traveled through the wind, through the night, across the boundary between life and death, reaching some distant and warm place.

Just like before.

Like that night illuminated by the firelight, a child squatted by the campfire, dipping a blade of grass in water and gently dripping it onto the mouth of a small snake.

And that fire never went out again.

Her last thought was of light.

It wasn't the blue light that burst forth from her body, but another, descending from an extremely high place, like a gentle river, flowing through the smoke, dust, and crumbling rocks, finding her precisely. She felt herself become lighter , as light as a scale, as light as a speck of dust blown by the wind, as light as a drop of dew sliding off a leaf. Then, she began to rise.

At first, it was slow, as if something was still tugging at the tip of her tail. It was the weight of the earth, the entanglement of blood, soil, and roots. She could feel the mountains rising and falling beneath her, the rivers meandering like silver threads, and the scorched earth breathing anew. Then, one by one, those entanglements loosened, like people seeing someone off untying their ropes on the shore. She rose higher and higher, through the clouds, through the cold winds of the high altitude, through the halos of starlight.

Her body remained on the earth. She could see it, like a winding mountain range, lying quietly on the land it once protected. The blood had long since drained, the scales were faded, yet its outline remained so clear, like an inscription etched into the earth. She gazed at it, her heart strangely calm. It was no longer her cage, but merely a worn-out garment.

She reformed herself in the starlight. No longer a colossal serpentine form, but a single, intensely bright blue light, like a tear collected by the heavens. That light emanated from the depths of her soul, gentle yet resolute, neither scorching nor dazzling. It slowly rose to the highest point of the night sky, finding the spot that had long been reserved for her.

Sam was there. Red Rock was there. Ears of Grain were there. The hunters were there. All the souls that had once ascended to the sky were there. They surrounded her, like a group of long-lost friends reunited. Suddenly, she felt that she was no longer alone.

Countless tiny lights emanated from her body, like dandelion seeds, drifting in all directions. Within each light lay a voice, an unspoken word, a melody carried away by the wind. She heard it. She heard the hunter's boy humming a tune by the campfire, broken and incoherent, yet so warm it brought tears to her eyes. She heard Sui's dying whisper, "I'm not afraid of pain." She heard Sam's roar as he charged at the monster, and Red Rock's last words before he collapsed. Those voices converged, like a river flowing through the starry sky, coming from a very, very far place, and flowing away to a very, very far place.

Then, she heard that melody.

It was the song people sang around the campfire when she first became known. The melody was ancient, so ancient that even she had forgotten the lyrics, but she recognized it as soon as the tune started. It was a song to welcome the gods, and also a song to bid farewell. It had been sung by generation after generation, burned by war, soaked by tears, and now it was slowly rising from the earth, passing through the night, and reaching where she was now.

It is the people on earth who are singing.

In the aftermath of the war, people stood by the river, on the mountaintop, beside the newly cultivated fields, gazing up at the blue star that had suddenly appeared. There were no commands, no agreements, but when the first person softly hummed that ancient song, the second joined in, then the third, the fourth. The sound was faint, like a candle flame in the wind, yet it could not be dispelled. It crossed the ruins, the new graves, the land where no crops had yet grown, rising all the way to the depths of the heavens.

She heard it.

The serpent's soul trembled slightly among the stars. She had no eyes, yet she felt a long-lost warmth. She had no mouth, yet she seemed to be smiling. She had no hands, yet she seemed to be embracing those on the ground who looked up at her.

It turns out, she was never truly alone.

She remembered that evening many, many years ago. She had just awoke from the ice, pitifully small, too weak even for a jackal. A child's voice pierced the silence: "Father! There's a little snake over there!" Then she was led back to a hut with a campfire burning. The firelight shone on her, warming her. A boy squatted by the fire, dipping a blade of grass in water and dripping it to her lips.

That was the first time she had heard a human melody. The boy was humming a tune with indistinct lyrics, his words broken and fragmented.

At that moment, the tune started playing again.

Through long and lonely years, through battlefields of blood and fire, through unspeakable sorrow and regret, it has sounded again.

This time, she didn't miss it.

A pale blue star twinkled softly in the night sky, like a drop of water finally falling into the sea. Its halo rippled outwards, gently covering the earth. The riverbank was filled with lights, one after another, like stars fallen from the sky, drifting away with the current. Within each light lay a wish. That wish traveled through the wind, through the night, across the boundary between life and death, reaching its destined place.

In the distance, that familiar melody still plays.

And she could finally rest assured and hand over that fire to everyone on earth.
