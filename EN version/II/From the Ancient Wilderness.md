#  From the Ancient Wilderness

Ancient times—time is the measure of mythology.

This beast is no ordinary beast. Its fangs bear the rust of the last moon; its shoulder blades rise, already taking the shape of mountains. This land has never been named, has no boundary markers, no furrows, no temple ruins . The moment you are bitten is, in fact, the first time this land reacts to you—not with hatred, not with punishment, but simply with contact. In its long geological history, you were nothing more than a fleeting itch on its skin. It responds to your intrusion with its fangs, just as rocks respond to rain with weathering. Pain is the oldest form of communication here, far more reliable than language. Your blood seeps into the soil and will grow into plants you will never recognize the following spring. Those plants need no names.

Beyond the ruins—time is the embers of history.

Seven years have passed since the plague. The village has mostly recovered, the newly built roofs patchwork of tiles of different colors. People no longer talk about the dead, as if they've signed a silent pact. You live alone on the far east side of the village, raising a few sheep. That day, you went to chop firewood on the north slope. The beast had actually been following you for a long time—it was thin, its ribs clearly visible, another survivor of the plague. When it pounced, you didn't even scream. As its fangs pierced your forearm, you looked down at it, and it looked back at you. You suddenly realized: you were both living with something more real than words. You walked back to the village, bleeding, encountering no one along the way, and intending not to tell anyone. You simply washed your wound, wrapped it tightly with strips of cloth, and continued chopping wood. The earth was never merciful because you spared it from the plague—you never expected it to be merciful. You are just living, living like that skinny beast, biting each day with your fangs, not letting it slip away easily.

History—time is the dust left after civilization has crumbled.

Humanity has regressed to the age of settlements. Not because of war, not because of climate change, but because everyone is tired—tired of screens, contracts, virtual identities, and endless "optimization." You choose to become a "returner," settling on the edge of the coniferous forest at 47 degrees north latitude. This land doesn't recognize your ID card, your social security number, or any credit you accumulated in another civilization. It only recognizes your footprints and body temperature. The beast, the former master of this area, has been observing you for three months and finally decided to give you a warning. When its fangs pierce your flesh, you don't feel fear, but an almost solemn acceptance. This land finally responds to you—not through network signals, not through monetary transactions, but through pure force and pain. You kneel on the moss, blood dripping into the humus layer of pine needles, the smell intense and real. You suddenly smile. Because you finally understand that true belonging is not about being protected, but about being responded to—even if that response is tearing you apart.
