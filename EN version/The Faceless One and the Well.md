#  The Faceless One and the Well

Conditions for transformation

Not everyone is qualified to become a Faceless One. Transformation can only occur during one's lifetime—after experiencing extreme suffering, if one still harbors a transcendent desire, one can choose to transform into a Faceless One at a certain critical point. Most who die unjustly become evil spirits; only a few with the potential can be transformed. Xin's forceful intervention in the laws of life and death comes at a price, therefore the number of Faceless One transformation slots is extremely limited. It can be understood this way: the Faceless Ones are Xin's last line of defense established before her death. They are not as grand as Heroic Spirits, but they are concrete enough—each Faceless One corresponds to a soul that has been saved.

Faceless

Every Faceless One's consciousness is linked to the same place, called the "Faceless Realm." If necessary, you can consider all Faceless Ones as the same person—because their consciousnesses are interconnected, they can share feelings, and they can substitute for one another. When a Faceless One dies, their memories and experiences do not disappear but flow into the Faceless Realm, becoming the shared legacy of all subsequent Faceless Ones. Therefore, every newly transformed Faceless One carries the memories of all previous Faceless Ones—the pain, the protection, the nameless sacrifices. This might explain why Faceless Ones are always silent—they carry too much and have no strength left to speak.

The faceless are socially awkward, unclean, and unattractive. They are seen as freaks, monsters, and omens of misfortune. But if you could see into their eyes through the mask, you would find no hatred, only a bottomless sorrow—because they have seen that well.

well

Legend has it that children with exceptionally strong spiritual abilities will dream of a well. The well is endlessly deep, its water like a mirror, yet the reflections are always distorted. In their dreams, the child stands beside the well, looking down not at their own face, but at the silhouette of a stranger, or a blurry form of countless overlapping figures. This dream recurs repeatedly, sometimes accompanied by severe headaches, auditory hallucinations, and visual hallucinations. These children are called "Children of the Well."

What exactly is the well? No one knows the exact answer. Some say it's a crack in the world's consciousness, some say it's the remains of ancient gods, some say it's the connection between the mirror world and the phenomenal world. But one thing is certain: once you step into this well, there is no turning back. It's like fate itself—you can only stand on the edge and watch, you can't jump in. Those who jump in either become madmen, saints, or faceless beings.

Every evil spirit was once a dead one, every boundary marker etched with a tragedy. The sorrow stems from having seen wells so deep, so very deep. And the children of the well are precisely the most hopeful beings of this era—because they can still feel that sorrow that transcends the individual, almost a sorrow directed at fate. As long as someone dreams of that well, as long as someone lingers by its edge, gazing and questioning, this world has not truly died.

Spirituality never dries up; it is merely worn away by suffering. But the spirit is like well water—as long as the earth still has life, it will always spring up again after a drought.

The Faceless Ones are the most silent guardians in this world.

Legend has it that after experiencing extreme suffering, if a person still harbors a relentless desire to transcend, they can choose to transform into a Faceless One at a certain critical point in their life. This is not a sublimation, not evolution, and certainly not a reward—it is a last resort, a desperate measure, the last straw the soul grasps before utter collapse. After transformation, the Faceless Ones wear a mask called "Faceless." From then on, they have no face, no age, no gender—they are simply a group of beings that cannot be categorized.

The faceless are seen as freaks and ostracized. They cannot speak, or speak in a way that differs from others; they are socially inept, unable to understand conventional politeness and implications; they do not compromise, always reacting directly to injustice; they refuse to conform, even at the cost of isolation; they are unclean, or rather, have no time to care about physical hygiene; they are not beautiful—their appearance is often incomplete or discordant, because the transformation process does not heal physical wounds, but only preserves them. They walk among people as if walking in another world.

But the Faceless Ones are not truly indifferent. On the contrary, they are people of extreme gentleness. It's just that that gentleness is sealed beneath their masks, unable to be expressed or conveyed. They are unlike humans in every way, yet in every way more human than humans—because they retain the purest part of humanity: when a person loses their appearance, their identity, their social skills, and everything that can be defined by "personality," what remains is the core, the truth of the soul. The Faceless Ones endure two kinds of pain throughout their lives: first, the disaster that pushed them to the brink of transformation itself; and second, the utter isolation after transformation, unable to be understood by anyone.

Not everyone is qualified to become a Faceless One. Xin's forceful intervention in the laws of life and death comes at a price, so most who die unjustly become evil spirits, with only a few with the potential to be transformed. The consciousness of every Faceless One is linked to the same place—called the "Faceless Realm." If necessary, you can consider all Faceless Ones to be the same person, because their consciousnesses are interconnected, they can share feelings, and they can substitute for each other. This connection makes the Faceless Ones an invisible network, with each member existing for the same goal: to slow the death of this world.

The existence of the Faceless Ones is not publicly documented. No one knows where they come from, what their mission is, or when they will appear. You will only notice them at certain moments—a masked figure suddenly appearing, saving a child in a disaster, and then disappearing without a trace; or a figure wandering on the edge of a wasteland, holding back a surging tide of evil spirits, only to turn to ashes at dawn. They never try to save the world, because that is impossible—they do only one thing: delay the world's death as much as possible. Using their suffering as fuel, they keep the world breathing a little longer.

Children who can hear the call of the well, possessing exceptional spirituality, often become prime targets for evil spirits. In uncivilized city-states, they are frequently treated unjustly, seen as outcasts, monsters, or harbingers of doom. These children are either ostracized to death, forced to wander into the wilderness, or, at some critical point, transformed into faceless beings—or into evil spirits. The line between these two fates sometimes hinges solely on whether the child encounters a warm-hearted person or a cold, indifferent world.

Behind every Faceless One lies a story, and behind every story lies an unhealable wound. But they don't speak of those stories. They simply wear masks, walk silently, guard silently, and then vanish silently.
