#  go home

Lin Jing rarely goes home.

After becoming a spiritualist, his spirituality grew stronger day by day, like a lamp that grew brighter the night ahead, easily attracting wandering evil spirits. Fearing that these things would follow him to his family, he wrote home again and again, using the excuse of his demanding studies to postpone his return. His family believed him, thinking he was just working hard at the academy, and wrote back to remind him to take care of his health and not to ruin his spirit.

They didn't know the bitterness in his heart. Lin Jing didn't dare let them know either.

The well's murmur haunted him from morning till night. When he sat in the courtyard listening to lectures, the sound echoed faintly in his ears; when he walked along the road, the sound brushed against the back of his head, like a distant whisper. He found it difficult to concentrate, and his reactions were always a beat slower than others. While others had already completed a spiritual hand seal, his fingers had only just begun to lift halfway.

But he knew he was relatively unaffected. At least he could remain conscious and control himself.

Lin Jing was gentle by nature. The little girl he met when he first enrolled, who said it was so cool to sit next to him, kept coming to see him. He didn't chase her away, nor did he find it annoying. Children's intuition can be quite sharp sometimes; she always said that sitting next to him was the most comfortable, like a breeze blowing from a well in summer.

"Brother Lin Jing, what are you thinking about?"

The little girl sat on the stone steps, her short legs dangling over the edge. She looked up at him, her eyes bright and round, as if waiting for him to tell her an interesting story.

"I wasn't thinking about anything," Lin Jing said. "Why did you come out again today?"

“Mom’s not home, there’s no one here.” The little girl pouted. “She has to work for someone every day, and she leaves before dawn.”

Lin Jing was silent for a moment. He had heard that the child's mother was a commoner, and according to the academy's rules, ordinary people's family members were not allowed to enter the academy. So the little girl could only stay in the dormitory area outside the academy. Her mother couldn't come in, and her father wasn't there, so she was like a little sparrow with nowhere to land, running around in the corners of the academy's edge.

"Do you miss home?" Lin Jing asked.

"Yes." The little girl lowered her head and shuffled her toes on the ground. "I want to go home, but home is so far away, and it takes a long time to get there by car. My mom said she'll take me back to see my grandma when she's saved enough money."

She gestured in the air as if trying to grab something. "There's a really big jujube tree in the house. When I was little, I would climb up the tree to pick and eat jujubes. There's also a stream that you can see from the back door." She mimed wading in the water, stomping her feet back and forth a few times as if she were standing in that imaginary stream.

Lin Jing didn't speak, just sat quietly, occasionally leaning slightly towards her to let her know he was listening.

The little girl added, "My father died in an accident when I was very young. I don't remember what he looked like at all."

She spoke very softly, as if she were talking about something that no longer hurt.

Lin Jing was stunned.

He suddenly remembered his deceased parents, whose faces he had forgotten. He tried desperately to recall, but only vague images came to mind. No, not even images, just a blurry ray of light, a lingering warmth he couldn't grasp.

Something bad surged up. Deep within his consciousness, the water in the well seemed to be stirred, and the persistent murmurs suddenly became sharp, as if someone were scraping at his brain. Lin Jing felt that familiar irritability rise from his chest, spreading to his limbs, his fingertips tingling slightly.

It took him a lot of effort to push it down, like pressing a rolling stone back to the bottom of the water with both hands.

"I have something to do, I'll head back first." Lin Jing stood up, trying to keep her voice steady.

The little girl nodded and then reminded him, "I'll come find you to play again tomorrow."

Lin Jing responded and turned to walk towards his courtyard. Only after closing the gate did his shoulders slump slightly. A breeze blew in from the wall, carrying the chill of late autumn.

He went inside and sat back down at the table. There were several sheets of white paper and a half-finished glass of cold tea on the table. Lin Jing picked up a pen and began to scribble. The hurried, chaotic lines resembled a swarm of centipedes crawling out from under the pen tip. He twisted his wrist, the pen moving back and forth across the paper, sometimes almost tearing it apart. Layers of black lines covered each other, gradually piling up into a suffocating tangled mess.

He drew faster and faster, with increasing force. The lines and blank spaces on the paper became indistinguishable, leaving only a deep gray expanse. The pen strokes were so heavy that several thin, elongated cuts appeared on the paper, the cracks spreading outwards. He gripped the pen tightly, his knuckles turning white, as if he wanted to break it.

He finally stopped after an unknown amount of time. The paper was unusable, as if it had been torn by something. Lin Jing looked down at the tangled mess of thread and felt something in her heart slowly loosen a little. Not much, but enough to breathe again.

He knew that his total psychic energy was increasing rapidly for the same reason. Those things that couldn't be suppressed were looking for an outlet, and each time he forced them out of his body onto the paper, to his fingertips, to the edge of that well.

Then he got up and sat down under the old tree in the courtyard. The wind blew withered leaves to his feet, and he glanced down at them. He had never mentioned the matter of concealing the abyss's spiritual power to anyone. His method was to pour most of the spiritual power into the well, like pouring river water back to its source, making what was underwater appear as just a thin layer.

What he usually shows is just the tip of the iceberg.

The spiritual energy of the abyss possesses a unique aura and characteristics. It is a bone-chilling cold and black flames, so cold that it freezes the air, yet as intense as burning ink. These characteristics are so distinct that anyone with discerning eyes can recognize them. He cannot allow anyone to see them, cannot allow anyone to sense them; he can only suppress them into the well, covering them with more ordinary spiritual traces.

But the price he paid was that he always felt unwell. Not from illness, but a weakness that welled up from within, like a fragile body trying to support a mismatched spirit. His weak body couldn't bear such a spirit; every day felt like trying to cram too much into a container that was too small.

Lin Jing looked up at the sky. The sky was grayish-white, without clouds or sun.

He suddenly remembered his grandmother.

She must be sweeping fallen leaves in the yard again. She'll gather the withered leaves into a pile, then look up at the alleyway, as if waiting for someone who hasn't returned in a long time.

Lin Jing closed her eyes.

He had to hold on. He wanted to live, he had desires, and he didn't want to die quietly like this. No matter what was in the well, no matter how much longer his body could hold on, he didn't want to collapse now.

When he opened his eyes again, there was no flame in them, only a still pool of water.
