The world certainly experiences spiritual exploitation, though the methods vary. These include encouraging procreation, creating a narrow path for survival, sacrificial offerings, deification to provide faith, or even outright, covert killing. How is this exploitation precise? By effortlessly exploiting human greed and instinct. Examples include pig-butchering scams, schools, role model propaganda, and creating competition. Commercial trade is another common method. Ultimately, humans have limited needs: food, drink, clothing, hygiene, and necessities. The ultimate goal is to harvest spiritual resources and extract value. Here, people are not living beings, but rather symbols of value to be extracted. They are tools of production, paid wages, and then exploited again to create a self-sufficient cycle. The purpose of this exploitation is, of course, to satisfy higher-level desires, such as longevity, power, dominance, and lasting fame.

Can deities not feed on spirits? Yes, they can. This feeding is very subtle. They don't kill living beings directly, but periodically plunder their spirits, causing them to become weak for a period of time as a price.

After the cobblestone path turned the third bend, Lin Jing saw the Holy Infant Hall.

It was a gray-white domed building standing at the intersection of the East Market and the inner city's main road. Above the door was inscribed a line of small characters: "May the people prosper and the gods be forever blessed." The main door was open, and the cries of infants came from inside, mixed with a sweet, cloying incense, so strong that it made one's nostrils ache.

He could have taken a detour. The academy was to the north, and the Holy Infant Church to the east; he could have easily avoided them by walking straight along the main road. But he tripped over something—actually, the tip of his shoe had kicked a cloth tiger, made of faded red cloth with crooked stitches, and only two whiskers remained. He bent down and picked it up; the cloth tiger's belly was bulging, vaguely stuffed with grass seeds, and it felt heavy.

A boy of about seven or eight years old crouched in the shadows of the side wall of the Holy Infant Church. He was thin, with deep-set eyes, but large eyes that were fixed intently on the cloth tiger in Lin Jing's hand. Lin Jing walked over and handed him the cloth tiger. The boy took it, clutched it to his chest, and suddenly said, "My mother has given birth again."

"Congratulations," Lin Jing said.

"She won't raise it." The boy tugged at the cloth tiger's ear. "She said the spiritual eye detected its low spiritual power, so she sent it to the hall in exchange for two food ration coupons. My dad registered it at the hall, saying our family has another member this year, so we'll get an extra half-liter of spiritual rice at the end of the month."

He spoke without expression, as if reciting a well-memorized scripture. Lin Jing squatted down, her gaze level with his. The boy's fingernails were full of black dirt, his sleeve was torn, and his exposed wrist was as thin as a reed.

"How old are you?" Lin Jing asked.

"Eight years old," the boy said. "My mother was fourteen when she gave birth to me. She didn't know how to raise me; I just grew up on my own. When I was hungry, I would go to the ancestral hall to mooch porridge, and when I was cold, I would crawl into a bag of sacred rice to sleep. This year, the ancestral hall said they didn't have enough sacred rice because I had three more younger siblings, and they were all taken away."

He turned the cloth tiger over; on its belly was a deep stain, like oil, or like a tear. "This is my sister's. She was taken away by a spirit last year and never came back. My father said the evil spirit ate her, and then left after it was full. Our whole family is fine."

Lin Jing's fingers were curled inside his sleeve. He knew that saying—evil spirits emerge from the void and leave after killing enough people. So every household tries to have as many children as possible, the more the better, so that they can survive if they encounter evil spirits. Population is a tool, a raw material, a field of spiritual energy. Gods need faith to strengthen themselves, and faith is squeezed bit by bit from the souls of the living.

"Are you scared?" Lin Jing heard herself ask.

The boy looked up, his large, somewhat empty eyes staring at him. "What are you afraid of? Evil spirits?" He thought for a moment. "Yes, I am. But being afraid is useless. The priest in the temple says that those whose spirits are taken away will become evil spirits, faceless beings, or sages. But how they become that is unknown. The priest says it's up to fate."

Lin Jing stood up. Another shrill, new cry came from inside the Holy Infant Hall. A young woman, no more than sixteen or seventeen years old, emerged from the doorway, carrying a swaddled infant. She hesitated on the steps. She wore the uniform grey robe issued by the Holy Infant Hall, her hair was haphazardly tied up, and there were still tear stains on her face. She placed the swaddled infant on the stone platform beside the steps, turned to go inside, took two steps, then turned back and reached out to touch the baby's face.

The baby was crying, and tiny fists were sticking out from the gaps in the cloth.

The woman knelt down and placed her index finger into the baby's hand. The baby grasped it, and the crying gradually subsided. She knelt there for a long time. Finally, she withdrew her finger, stood up, and walked into the hall without looking back. The hem of her gray robe trailed across the threshold, and the light inside swallowed her silhouette.

Lin Jing walked over. The swaddle on the stone platform was made of coarse cotton cloth, the edges worn and frayed. The baby's face was wrinkled, eyes closed, clenching its empty hand, lips moving. Next to it was another swaddle, twins. The two babies were placed side by side on the stone platform, like two pieces of merchandise awaiting registration.

A head popped out from the gatehouse of the Holy Infant Church. A middle-aged man in a steward's robe held a spirit pen and a booklet. "Here we go again?" He glanced in the direction of the young woman's retreating figure, then made a mark in the booklet. "Alright, registered. Numbers A73 and A74. Spiritual power to be tested."

"Where are their parents?" Lin Jing asked.

The steward looked up and examined his uniform badge, his tone becoming more polite. "The academy's elders. That was the mother just now. The father is unknown; he didn't come. The hall only accepts children, not inquires. More children mean more spiritual energy; the Sacred Flame is already burning enough."

Lin Jing looked at the two babies on the stone platform. Twins, one big and one small, the smaller one's cries were weaker, like a cat's meow. He touched the worn-out talisman in his sleeve, but ultimately didn't take it out. The talisman could ward off evil spirits, but it couldn't protect against the cold, hunger, or being registered as Jia 73 and Jia 74.

"The Kingdom of God encourages childbirth," the steward said, closing the booklet and dusting it off. "This is what the higher-ups have decided. More children mean more faith and more spirituality. But raising children..." He smiled faintly, as if stating something self-evident , "Whether or not to raise children is a matter for each family. The church only handles collection and recording. As you know, sir, people are free; they can have children if they want. However..."

He didn't finish speaking, then waved his hand and retreated back into the gatehouse.

Lin Jing turned and left. As he passed the stone platform, he heard the smaller of the twins suddenly stop crying. He glanced at it and saw the larger one extend its fist, which the smaller one clenched. The two infants, separated by their respective coarse cotton cloths, leaned against each other quietly.

Several white doves perched on the dome of the Church of the Infant, cooing softly. The sky was a deep blue, so blue it didn't seem like it harbored any evil spirits. But Lin Jing knew the void was omnipresent, just as she knew what those who were sucked away by spirits might become—evil spirits, faceless beings, sages. No one could clearly define the criteria for judgment, but it existed vaguely, like a scale hanging over everyone's head.

He walked out of the East Market. The cries from the Holy Infant Church behind him gradually faded into the distance, but he could still feel them echoing in his ears. The image of that eight-year-old boy crouching against the wall, the trembling shoulders of that young woman as she pulled her fingers out, and the tiny baby on the stone platform with its two fists clenched together.

The cloth tiger, its red thread faded, only two whiskers remained. The boy said it belonged to his sister.

Lin Jing's gaze fell on the tips of his boots. The spiritual dust had dissipated, revealing the bluish-gray leather beneath. Humans are free, but some suffering is man-made. He recalled the patterns on the Faceless Man's mask, and those eyes that flickered with ghostly fire. The Faceless Man had said then, "Don't expect one person to end everything."

But if everyone thinks like that, will the babies on the stone platform just be numbers forever?

He continued walking forward. At the end of the official road stood the White Pagoda of Sacred Flame, burning day and night.
